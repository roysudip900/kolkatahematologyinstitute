> Aplastic anaemia is a rare but serious condition in which the bone marrow cannot make enough red cells, white cells and platelets, so all three counts fall. It is not a cancer, and it can often be treated with medicines that calm the immune system or, for some people, a transplant, so tiredness, bruising, bleeding and infections together need a hematologist without delay.

The word "anaemia" made her mother think iron tablets would fix it.

She is nineteen, in her second year at a college in Krishnanagar. For a month her gums have bled when she brushes, bruises have appeared on her legs without a knock, and she has had two fevers. Her blood report showed all three kinds of blood cells low. Her mother bought iron tablets from the chemist on the way home.

In Asansol, a man of fifty-five who has sprayed crops for years has felt more and more tired. A nosebleed would not stop, and the doctor found his counts low and sent him to a hematologist.

She wants to know whether she has blood cancer. He wants to know whether the sprays did this.

# What aplastic anaemia is

The bone marrow, the soft tissue inside the bones, holds stem cells that make red cells, white cells and platelets. In aplastic anaemia these stem cells are damaged, so the marrow makes too few of all three. Despite its name, it is more than anaemia, and it is also called bone marrow failure.

It can come on quickly or slowly, and it can be mild or serious. It is not a cancer.

# Why iron tablets will not help

Iron helps when the body is short of iron. In aplastic anaemia the marrow itself cannot make enough cells, so iron cannot fix it. Do not start or stop iron, tonics or any other medicine because of a blood report. Show the report to the hematologist first.

# Why the marrow test is needed

When all three counts are low, the hematologist needs to see the marrow itself to find out why. The bone marrow test takes a small sample from the back of the hip bone with the skin numbed. The test shows whether the marrow has too few cells, and helps find the cause.

# What causes it

The most common cause is the body's own defence system attacking the stem cells in the marrow. Other causes include some medicines, such as chemotherapy, and toxins or chemicals in the surroundings. Rarely, it runs in families.

# Signs of aplastic anaemia

- bleeding, most often from the gums, which people notice as bleeding from the mouth; nosebleeds and bruises can also happen
- fever, or often feeling feverish
- tiredness
- infections that last a long time

# What treatment can do

Treatment may include:

- blood and platelet transfusions, to ease the symptoms
- medicines that aim to stop the defence system from destroying the stem cells, such as antithymocyte globulin (ATG) and ciclosporin
- a newer medicine, eltrombopag, which can help the marrow make blood cells, sometimes given with them
- a blood or bone marrow transplant, which may cure aplastic anaemia in some people, but needs a donor and carries risks, such as infections and the donor cells attacking the body
- staying away from any toxin or chemical that may have caused it

People taking medicines that calm the defence system need to take steps to prevent infection. The hematologist checks them regularly, because aplastic anaemia can raise the risk of bleeding, leukaemia or other serious blood conditions.

# How the stories go on

The student's marrow test shows aplastic anaemia. The hematologist tells her it is not a cancer and that iron will not help. Her brother's blood is tested to see if he can be a donor, and she starts treatment.

The farmer's marrow test also shows aplastic anaemia. He starts treatment, his son takes over the spraying with a mask and gloves, and he learns which signs mean the hospital.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- a fever or shivering, above all during treatment
- bleeding that does not stop, or blood in vomit, urine or stools
- a sudden severe headache, which may be bleeding inside the head
- very pale, or breathless at rest

# Take these questions to your doctor

A hematologist leads the care of aplastic anaemia. Bring your reports and ask:

- How serious is it in my case?
- Is a transplant possible, and can my family be tested as donors?
- Which treatment do you suggest first, and why?
- How do I lower the chance of infections during treatment?
- Is there anything at home or at work I should stay away from?

# Common questions

## Is aplastic anaemia a blood cancer?

No. It is a failure of the bone marrow, not a cancer. But it is serious, and it can raise the risk of leukaemia, so regular check-ups matter.

## Will iron tablets or tonics help?

Not for aplastic anaemia, because the marrow itself cannot make enough cells. Do not start or stop them on your own. The hematologist will decide what you need.

## Can it be cured?

A transplant may cure it in some people, but it needs a donor and carries serious risks. Medicines that calm the defence system are the other main treatment. The hematologist explains which is right for you.
