She is fifty-nine and has just retired from teaching. Last year she was so tired she had to sit down halfway through cooking, her tongue was red and sore, and she had pins and needles in her feet. A blood test showed anaemia and very low vitamin B12. The doctor said she had pernicious anaemia and started injections. Within a few months she felt like herself again. When the next one was due, she was busy with her granddaughter's admission, and then it seemed unnecessary. Now, a year on, the tiredness is creeping back.

Her neighbour is fifty-two. He has had diabetes for ten years and takes his sugar tablet every day, along with a tablet for acidity that he started long ago and never stopped. He feels drained by evening. His B12 has come back low, and the word metformin was mentioned. He is worried that he will have to give up his diabetes medicine.

She wants to know why she should take injections if she feels well. He wants to know whether his tablets did this.

# What B12 and folate do

The body needs vitamin B12 and folate to make red blood cells. Without enough of either, it makes red cells that are too large and do not work properly, and anaemia follows: tiredness, breathlessness, a pale face. B12 also keeps the nerves healthy, which is why low B12 can cause tingling, unsteady walking and memory trouble. The part on the heart and nerves covers those signs.

B12 comes from fish, meat, eggs and milk. Folate comes from green vegetables, peas and chickpeas. To get B12 from food into the blood, the stomach adds a helper protein, and the gut takes the two in together. When any step fails, the body runs short.

# Why her injections cannot stop

In pernicious anaemia the body's own defence system attacks the cells in the stomach that make the helper protein. Without it, B12 from food cannot be taken in, however much fish or egg she eats. It is more common in women around sixty, in people with a family history, and in people with another condition of this kind, such as the white patches of vitiligo.

Because the problem is in the stomach, it does not go away when she feels better. The injections put B12 straight into the body, and most people with pernicious anaemia need them for the rest of their life. When they stop, the body's store slowly runs down and the anaemia, and the nerve trouble, can come back. Nerve damage left for a long time may not fully recover.

Pernicious anaemia also raises the risk of stomach cancer, which is one more reason to stay in regular touch with the doctor.

# Why his B12 ran low

B12 can run low for several reasons:

- eating little or no fish, meat, eggs or milk, as in some vegetarian and vegan diets
- an operation on the stomach, or a gut condition that stops it being absorbed
- some medicines taken for a long time, including acid tablets and metformin

His diabetes tablet and his acid tablet may both have played a part. He should not stop either one on his own. His doctor weighs the benefit of each medicine, replaces the B12, and checks it from time to time.

# Folate

The body keeps only a small store of folate, so it needs some every day from food. It can run short with a poor diet, with heavy drinking, with some gut conditions and medicines, and when the body needs more, as in pregnancy.

If you are planning a baby, ask your doctor about folic acid before you conceive. Low folate, and low B12 too, can raise the risk of a serious defect in the baby's brain and spine.

Folic acid is not started on its own until B12 has been checked. It can ease the anaemia so well that a hidden B12 shortage is missed, while the nerves go on being harmed.

# What treatment looks like

The doctor first finds out which vitamin is low and why. B12 is usually given as injections at first. After that, depending on the cause, it continues as tablets or as injections at regular intervals. Folate is usually given as tablets for a few months, and longer if the cause continues. Repeat blood tests show whether it is working.

How much, how often and for how long is decided for each person.

# How the stories go on

She goes back to her doctor and tells the truth about the missed injections. They restart, and she puts the dates on the calendar next to her granddaughter's school timetable. The doctor explains that this is now part of her life, like her spectacles, and that her regular checks matter.

His doctor gives him B12, keeps his diabetes under control, and reviews whether he still needs the acid tablet. Over the following weeks the evenings get easier.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- chest pain, or breathlessness while sitting or lying still
- fainting, or a racing heartbeat that will not settle
- sudden confusion, or sudden trouble walking, speaking or seeing
- sudden weakness or numbness of the face, an arm or one side of the body

# Take these questions to your doctor

A hematologist can help when the cause of low B12 is not clear, or when the nerves are affected. Take your reports and the strips of all your medicines, and ask:

- Is it B12, folate or both, and why is it low?
- Is this pernicious anaemia?
- Could any of my medicines be the cause?
- Do I need injections or tablets, and for how long?
- When should my blood be checked again?
- If I am planning a pregnancy, what should I take, and when?

# Common questions

## Why do I need B12 injections for life?

If the stomach cannot take in B12, as in pernicious anaemia, food and tablets cannot make up for it. Most people then need injections for the rest of their life, even when they feel well.

## Do vegetarians need B12 tablets?

People who eat little or no fish, meat, eggs or milk can run short of B12. Ask your doctor whether you need a blood test, and do not start supplements without advice.

## Can I take folic acid on my own?

Folic acid is often advised before and in early pregnancy, so ask your doctor. Otherwise, take it only if your doctor advises it after a blood test, because it can hide a B12 shortage.
