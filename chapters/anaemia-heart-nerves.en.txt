Three months after the baby came, her heart started to race.

It happens when she lifts the older child onto her hip, and on the stairs up from the market. At night, lying in the dark, she can hear it thudding in her ears. Some nights a cramp knots her calf, or her legs will not keep still until she gets up and walks. Her father had a heart attack at fifty-five, and she is sure this is the same thing starting. At a clinic near the station, the ECG comes back normal. She is told it is stress, and that she should rest. She goes home, lies down, and listens to her heart pound again.

In another part of the city, a grandmother of sixty-seven has had pins and needles in her feet for a year. She eats no fish, meat or eggs since her husband died, and drinks little milk. At night she keeps one hand on the wall on the way to the bathroom. She forgets whether she has taken her morning tablets, which include an acid tablet she has taken every day for years. Her son says her nerves are weak with age. The chemist sells her a nerve tonic.

Neither of them has had a blood count.

# Why low blood can feel like heart trouble

Hemoglobin is what carries oxygen in the blood. When there is too little of it, the body tries to make up the difference with speed. The heart beats faster and harder to move the blood it has, and you feel it as a racing or thudding heart, breathlessness on small effort, or dizziness when you stand up.

[figure: heart-pace]

The young mother's heart is healthy. It is working overtime because her blood is short of hemoglobin. The pregnancy, the blood she lost at the delivery, and meals eaten last and in a hurry had used up her iron.

An ECG looks at the heart's rhythm. It does not measure hemoglobin, so a normal ECG cannot tell you whether you have anaemia. A simple blood count can.

Severe anaemia left alone for long does strain the heart, so it is worth finding early.

# Why the nerves need vitamin B12

The body needs vitamin B12 to make red blood cells and to keep the nerves working. When B12 runs low, both suffer at once. The blood becomes anaemic, and the nerves start to misfire: pins and needles, numbness in the feet or hands, unsteady walking, trouble with eyesight or memory, low mood.

Vitamin B12 comes mainly from fish, meat, eggs and milk. Someone who eats little of these, like the grandmother, can slowly run short. So can people whose stomach cannot absorb it, and some people who take certain medicines for a long time, including acid tablets and metformin for diabetes.

This kind of nerve trouble needs to be found early. Many of the symptoms improve with treatment, but nerve damage that is left for a long time can become permanent.

# Check the heart and the blood together

A pounding heart can also come from the thyroid or from a real problem with the heart's rhythm. Tingling feet can come from diabetes and many other causes. So keep the heart and nerve tests, and ask for a blood count as well. Anaemia is common, the test is simple, and the treatment is usually straightforward once the cause is known.

Other signs are worth mentioning to your doctor too:

- looking paler than usual
- a ringing or buzzing in the ears
- legs that cramp, or will not keep still, at night
- feeling cold when others do not, especially in the hands and feet
- a sore, red tongue, or sores at the corners of the mouth
- headaches that keep coming back
- more hair than usual coming out in the comb, or nails that break easily or curve like a spoon
- a craving to chew ice, clay or chalk
- trouble swallowing, or food that tastes strange

# How the stories go on

The young mother sees another doctor, who listens to her heart and then asks about the delivery and how much blood she lost. A blood count shows her hemoglobin is low. Her doctor looks for the reason and starts treatment. Over the weeks that follow, the thudding at night fades, and so do the cramps. She still has her heart checked once a year, because of her father.

The grandmother's grandson takes her to a doctor, who asks what she eats and which tablets she takes. Her blood count is low, and so is her vitamin B12. She starts treatment and is followed up closely. The tingling eases slowly. Her doctor is honest that some of it may stay, because it went on for a long time.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- chest pain, or breathlessness while sitting or lying still
- fainting, or a racing heartbeat that will not settle
- sudden weakness or numbness of the face, an arm or one side of the body, or slurred speech
- sudden confusion, or sudden trouble walking

# Take these questions to your doctor

If your heart races or your hands and feet tingle, see your doctor, and if a blood count shows anaemia without a clear cause, ask to see a hematologist. Ask:

- Could anaemia be causing this?
- Can we do a blood count, as well as the tests for my heart or nerves?
- Should my vitamin B12 be checked?
- Could any of my regular medicines be lowering my B12?
- If it is anaemia, what is causing it?

# Common questions

## Can anaemia cause palpitations?

Yes. When hemoglobin is low, the heart beats faster to move enough oxygen, and many people feel it pounding or racing. Palpitations have other causes too, so see a doctor and ask for a blood count along with the heart tests.

## Can low vitamin B12 cause tingling in the hands and feet?

Yes. Low vitamin B12 can cause pins and needles, numbness and unsteadiness, often together with anaemia. It needs treatment early, because long-standing nerve damage may not fully recover.

## Should I take vitamin B12 or nerve tonics on my own?

It is better to have a blood test first. Your doctor needs to know whether B12 is really low and why, and then decides on the treatment. A tonic can delay finding the real cause.
