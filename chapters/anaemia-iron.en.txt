# Who runs short of iron

Iron deficiency is the most common kind of anaemia. Those most at risk are young children, especially under two, girls and women who have periods, and women during pregnancy and after birth. Heavy periods and pregnancy are among the most common reasons in women. In pregnancy, it is most often because the diet has too little iron.

Iron also runs out when blood is lost slowly from the stomach or bowel, for example from painkillers such as ibuprofen and aspirin taken for a long time. Sometimes the body cannot take iron in, because of a problem in the gut.

The main Anaemia page explains why low iron in a man needs looking into.

# Why the doctor looks for the reason first

Iron tablets put iron back, but they cannot stop it leaking out. So the doctor first asks why it ran short: how heavy your periods are, what you eat, whether you take painkillers, whether you have stomach trouble. The blood tests usually include the blood count and the iron stores (ferritin). Sometimes a stool test looks for hidden blood, or a specialist is needed.

When the reason is heavy periods, they can be treated, and treating them is part of treating the anaemia.

# Taking the tablets

Take them the way your doctor tells you. These help:

- Taking the tablet with food or soon after can ease an upset stomach.
- Tea, coffee and milk in large amounts make it harder for the body to take in iron, so keep them away from the tablet. A glass of orange juice, or lemon on your food, may help.
- Iron tablets often make stools darker than usual, and that is usually nothing to worry about. They can also cause constipation, loose stools, tummy pain, heartburn and feeling sick.
- If a side effect is hard to bear, keep taking the tablets and tell your doctor. There is more than one kind of iron tablet, and iron can also be given through a vein. The doctor decides.
- Keep iron tablets locked away from children. Too many can be fatal for a small child.

# How long it takes

Refilling the iron stores often takes three to six months of tablets. The doctor will repeat the blood test over those months to see the hemoglobin and the iron coming back. Keep taking them for as long as the doctor says, even when you feel better, and keep the follow-up tests.

# When the hemoglobin does not rise

If you have taken iron for months and the hemoglobin is still low, go back to the doctor rather than buying more tablets from the chemist. Some of the reasons can be:

- the tablets were stopped, or taken on and off, because of side effects
- they were taken with tea or milk
- blood is still being lost, from heavy periods or the stomach
- the gut is not taking the iron in
- it was never iron deficiency, as with thalassemia or hemoglobin E carriers, or low vitamin B12 or folate

The government's anaemia programme also sends people whose hemoglobin does not improve, despite taking iron, for more tests. Do not stop the tablets on your own while you wait.

# In pregnancy

In India, hemoglobin is checked at the antenatal visits, and iron is given through the programme. Take it as advised. If you know you are a carrier of thalassemia or hemoglobin E, tell the doctor, who will decide about iron for you.

# How the stories go on

The young mother tells the doctor about the dark stools and the stomach. The doctor explains that darker stools are common with iron and what to watch for, changes the time she takes the tablet, and sends her to have her heavy periods looked at. She keeps going.

The teacher's doctor asks about her periods for the first time and moves her tablet away from her tea. Her periods are treated. At the next test, months later, her hemoglobin has finally moved.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- chest pain, breathlessness at rest, or fainting
- stools that are black and tar-like, or red, or have blood in them, or vomiting blood
- very heavy bleeding from anywhere
- a pregnant woman with anaemia who becomes breathless, dizzy or very weak
- a child may have swallowed iron tablets. Go at once, even if the child seems well.

# Take these questions to your doctor

- What do you think made my iron run short?
- How should I take the tablets, and what should I keep away from them?
- What should I do if the tablets upset my stomach?
- When will my blood be checked again, and how long will I need iron?
- Can my heavy periods be treated?

# Common questions

## Why are my stools dark on iron tablets?

Iron tablets often make stools darker than usual, and that is usually nothing to worry about. Stools that are black and tar-like, or red, or have blood in them can mean bleeding and need a hospital the same day.

## Can I take iron tablets with tea?

Tea, coffee and milk make it harder for the body to take in iron. Keep them away from the tablet, as your doctor advises.

## I have taken iron for months. Why is my hemoglobin still low?

It may be that the tablets were missed or taken with tea, that blood is still being lost, that the gut is not taking the iron in, or that the anaemia is not from low iron at all. Go back to the doctor to find out which.
