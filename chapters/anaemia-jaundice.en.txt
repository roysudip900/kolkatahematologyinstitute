> When someone is pale and tired and their eyes turn yellow, the red cells may be breaking down faster than the body can replace them. This is called haemolytic anaemia. Its causes include infections such as malaria, some medicines, the body attacking its own red cells, and inherited conditions such as thalassemia and sickle cell disease. Jaundice can also come from the liver, so it always needs a doctor soon, and dark urine, breathlessness or a yellow newborn baby in the first day needs a hospital at once.

The mother wants to know whether this is liver jaundice. The woman wants to know why her haemoglobin is low.

# Why anaemia and jaundice can come together

Red cells live for up to about four months, and then the spleen removes them. In haemolytic anaemia, red cells are destroyed faster than the bone marrow can replace them. The person becomes pale, tired and weak. As the broken red cells are cleared, a yellow substance called bilirubin builds up, and the eyes and skin turn yellow. The spleen or liver may become larger.

Jaundice also has other causes, such as hepatitis, liver disease from alcohol, gallstones and pancreatitis. That is why the doctor looks for the reason, and why home remedies for "kamla" can delay the right treatment.

# What can cause it

- infections, including malaria, which can break red cells and cause jaundice and dark urine
- some medicines
- the body's defences attacking its own red cells (autoimmune haemolytic anaemia)
- inherited blood conditions, such as sickle cell disease, thalassemia, and G6PD deficiency, in which an enzyme that protects red cells is low
- reactions to a blood transfusion
- in a newborn baby, a different blood group from the mother's

Haemolytic anaemia can come on quickly or slowly, and can be mild or serious.

# Signs

- pale skin, tiredness, weakness or dizziness
- yellow eyes or skin
- dark urine, like tea or cola
- breathlessness or a fast heartbeat
- a swelling or heaviness under the left ribs, from a larger spleen

On brown skin, yellow is easier to see in the whites of the eyes.

# How it is found

The doctor asks about fever, medicines, travel and family history, examines the eyes, skin, spleen and liver, and asks for blood tests. Urine tests, and sometimes a bone marrow test or genetic tests, help find the cause.

# What treatment can do

Treatment depends on the cause.

- An infection such as malaria is treated quickly.
- If a medicine is the cause, the doctor changes or stops it. Do not stop or change any medicine on your own.
- When the body attacks its own red cells, medicines that calm the immune system are used.
- A blood transfusion may be needed in an emergency, when the anaemia is severe, or regularly in thalassemia major, where the body cannot make enough healthy blood. It does not treat the cause, which still has to be found and treated.
- For some people, removing the spleen or a bone marrow transplant is considered.
- Mild haemolytic anaemia may need no treatment, only check-ups.

Untreated severe haemolytic anaemia can strain the heart, so it should not be left alone.

# Jaundice in a newborn baby

Yellow skin and eyes are common in newborn babies and are usually harmless, getting better within about two weeks. But jaundice in the first day of life, or jaundice in a baby who is very sleepy, not feeding, floppy, has a fever or feels cold, needs a hospital at once. One cause is the baby's blood group differing from the mother's, which breaks down the baby's red cells.

# How the stories go on

The girl's mother takes her to the health centre instead of using home remedies. Her blood test shows anaemia and a malaria test is positive. She is treated in hospital, and her colour and energy come back over the next weeks.

The woman's hematologist finds that her body is attacking its own red cells. She starts treatment to calm her immune system, has regular blood tests, and is told which signs mean she must come back at once.

# Go to a hospital now if

Go at once, and do not drive yourself, if there is jaundice with:

- dark, red or cola-coloured urine, or very little urine
- breathlessness, chest pain, or fainting
- a high fever, confusion or fits
- very pale skin and extreme tiredness
- bleeding for no reason

For a baby: jaundice in the first day of life, or jaundice with a baby who is very sleepy or hard to wake, not feeding, floppy or stiff, jerking, has a fever or feels cold, has no wet nappies, or is breathing fast or with difficulty.

# Take these questions to your doctor

- Is this jaundice from the liver or from red cells breaking down?
- What is causing my red cells to break down?
- Could any of my medicines be the cause? Who will decide whether to change it?
- Do I need tests for malaria or for an inherited condition?
- Do other family members need testing?
- Which signs mean I should come back at once?

# Common questions

## Is every jaundice a liver problem?

No. Jaundice can also come from red cells breaking down too fast. When jaundice comes with paleness and tiredness, tell the doctor, who will look for the cause.

## Can home remedies for "kamla" treat it?

They cannot treat the cause, and they can delay treatment for something serious, such as malaria or severe anaemia. See a doctor soon.

## Is jaundice in my newborn dangerous?

Usually not. Most newborn jaundice is harmless and fades within about two weeks. Jaundice in the first day, or with sleepiness, poor feeding, fever or a baby who feels cold, needs a hospital at once.
