The note came home folded inside a maths copy.

The girl is twelve, in class seven at a school near Krishnanagar. Last week a health team pricked every child's finger. Her slip says her hemoglobin is low, and the class teacher gave her a strip of blue iron tablets. The tablet made her feel sick for two mornings, and now the strip stays at the bottom of her school bag. Her grandmother says every girl is pale at this age and it will settle as she grows. Nobody has written down when the retest is.

In a lane in Howrah, a father has a bottle of iron syrup from the anganwadi. The anganwadi worker told him his three-year-old son's hemoglobin is low. He bought a tonic from the chemist as well, so the boy would get stronger faster. This week the boy has a fever and loose motions, and the father does not know whether to give the syrup, the tonic, both, or neither.

Both families have a slip of paper that says low, and no idea what they are meant to do next.

# What the test is

Under the government's anaemia programme, Anemia Mukt Bharat, the school health team (RBSK) tests children for anaemia in schools and anganwadi centres. A drop of blood from a finger goes into a small digital machine, which reads the hemoglobin, the part of the red cells that carries oxygen. When it is low, that is anaemia.

The finger-prick quickly finds the children who need care. Blood from a vein, taken at a health centre, gives the more exact measure.

# When a girl looks pale

A child with low hemoglobin can look quite well, or only a little pale and tired. Girls who have started their periods are among those most likely to be anaemic, so a low result in a girl needs looking after. Anaemia can affect a child's energy, attention and schoolwork.

# The tablets and the syrup

The programme gives iron with folic acid: a tablet for school children, pink for the younger ones and blue for the older ones, and a syrup for small children. A child with anaemia gets iron as treatment. Give it exactly the way the health team or health worker explains, for as long as they say.

Iron often upsets the stomach at first. Feeling sick, tummy ache and constipation are common, and they often fade over the first few weeks. If the tablet makes your child feel sick, the programme suggests taking it after a meal. Iron can also turn stools dark, and the programme says this is expected. If the iron troubles your child, tell the teacher or the health worker instead of quietly stopping it. Bad vomiting, belly pain that does not go, or a rash needs a doctor.

Do not add a tonic or more iron from the chemist on your own. Ask the doctor first. Too much iron can be fatal for a small child, so keep all iron, the syrup too, locked away from children.

# If your child falls ill, or already has a blood condition

During an illness such as fever, diarrhoea or pneumonia, the programme holds back its routine iron, and a doctor decides about iron treatment. If your child falls ill while on the iron, ask the health worker or your child's doctor what to do, and do not decide on your own.

If your child has thalassemia major or has needed blood transfusions, tell the school health team. The programme does not give its routine iron to these children, and their own doctor decides about iron.

# The retest

The programme aims to follow every child until the anaemia has gone. A child found low is tested again after a course of iron, at a school visit or at the nearest health centre (Ayushman Arogya Mandir). When it is due depends on how low the first result was, so ask the health worker for the date and write it down.

If the hemoglobin has not risen, even though the child took the iron well, the programme sends the child to a bigger hospital to find out why. A child whose first result is very low is sent there too.

Show the slip to your child's own doctor too if your child seems very pale or tired, or if you are unsure what it means.

# How the stories go on

At the parent-teacher meeting, the girl's mother shows the class teacher the strip of tablets. The teacher tells her the sickness often fades and the tablet can go after a meal. The retest date goes on the kitchen calendar. At the retest, the health worker will see whether the hemoglobin has moved and decide the next step.

The father takes his son to a doctor for the fever, with the syrup and the tonic in a bag. The doctor treats the fever first and tells him what to do about the iron, and when.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- your child is very pale, very drowsy, or too tired to eat or play
- your child is breathless at rest, has chest pain, or faints
- after the iron, your child's face or lips swell, or breathing becomes hard
- stools are black and tar-like, or red, or have blood in them
- a small child may have swallowed iron tablets or syrup meant for someone else. Go at once, even if the child seems well

# Take these questions to your doctor

Take the school slip and the tablets or syrup to the health worker or your child's doctor, and ask:

- Is my child's result a little low, or a lot?
- How should my child take the iron, and for how long?
- What should we do if it upsets my child's stomach?
- When and where is the retest?
- My child has a fever or loose motions. Should the iron go on?

# Common questions

## The school says my child's hemoglobin is low. Is it serious?

It needs looking after. The programme gives iron and tests again, and sends a child with a very low result to a hospital. Ask how low your child's result is.

## My child feels sick after the school iron tablet. Should I stop it?

Do not stop it on your own. Tell the teacher or the health worker. Feeling sick is common at first and often fades, and taking the tablet after a meal may help.

## Should the iron syrup go on during a fever or diarrhoea?

Ask the health worker or your child's doctor before the next dose. During illnesses such as fever, diarrhoea or pneumonia, the programme holds back its routine iron, and a doctor decides about iron treatment.
