For two years, she blamed the stairs.

Their flat is on the third floor, with no lift. By the second landing she has to stop and hold the railing. She is thirty-four. She tells herself it is age, or the housework, or the heat. She eats last, after everyone else, and ends the meal with a cup of tea. A neighbour says beetroot. Her mother says pomegranate. Nobody says blood test.

In another home, a retired man of sixty-two has a routine check up. The report says his hemoglobin is low. The chemist at the corner hands him an iron tonic. He takes it, feels a little better, and tells no one.

Both of them have anaemia. For one, iron is the right answer, though not the whole answer. For the other, iron is hiding something. This page is about the one word that matters behind every low hemoglobin: why.

# What low hemoglobin means

Blood is red because of hemoglobin, the part of the red cell that carries oxygen from your lungs to the rest of your body. Think of it as a fleet of lorries, delivering oxygen street by street. Anaemia means too few lorries on the road, so you tire quickly, and stairs leave you breathless.

Anaemia is not a disease in itself. It is a sign that something else is wrong, the way a fever is a sign. A low number on the report tells you the lorries are short. It does not tell you where they went.

# Why she is tired

Her body is running out of iron, and without iron it cannot build hemoglobin. This is the most common kind of anaemia there is.

Look at where her iron goes. Her periods have been heavy for years, one of the commonest reasons a woman runs short of iron, and she has never thought it worth mentioning. She eats last and eats little. And tea with a meal makes it harder for the body to take in the iron in the food.

The beetroot and the pomegranate are good food. But they cannot answer the real question, which is where her iron is going. That takes a doctor, a blood test, and a conversation about her periods that nobody has ever had with her.

# Why his tonic is not enough

A man of his age does not usually run short of iron because of his food. When his iron is low, the first thing a doctor asks is whether he is losing blood somewhere, slowly, without seeing it. The stomach and the bowel are the usual places: an ulcer, piles, or painkillers taken for years for an aching knee. Less often it is a growth, the kind of thing worth finding early.

The tonic will push his hemoglobin up. That is the trouble with it. It tidies the report and leaves the leak. It is like mopping the floor and never looking at the tap.

# When iron is the wrong answer

There is a third story, and in Bengal it is a common one. A young woman has taken iron tonics on and off since her school days, because her hemoglobin is always a little low. It never comes up.

She may never have been short of iron at all. Many healthy people in Bengal are carriers of thalassemia or of hemoglobin E. Their red cells are small and their hemoglobin runs slightly low, which on a report can look just like a lack of iron. But it is not the same thing, and iron does nothing for it. A particular blood test tells the two apart.

And there are other causes too, from too little vitamin B12 to kidney disease, each needing its own answer.

# How the stories go on

She finally shows her report to a doctor, who asks about her periods. No one had asked before. She is given iron, and treatment for the bleeding. The tablets upset her stomach at first, and she is tempted to stop on the day she feels better. Her doctor explains why she must not: feeling better comes long before the body's store of iron is full again. Months later, the stairs are only stairs.

He mentions the tonic to his doctor at last, and is sent for tests to find where the blood is going. Whatever they show, he is better placed than he was, because now someone is looking.

And the young woman has one test, learns she is a carrier, and is told she can leave the iron alone. The thalassemia chapter on this site tells the rest of her story.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- chest pain, or breathlessness while sitting or lying still
- fainting, or a racing heartbeat that will not settle
- vomiting blood, stools that are black like tar, or blood in the stool
- heavy bleeding from anywhere that does not stop
- a pregnant woman with anaemia who becomes breathless, dizzy or very weak

# Take these questions to your doctor

If your report says your hemoglobin is low, do not settle for a tonic and a guess. Take it to your doctor. If the cause is not clear, or the number will not rise, ask to see a hematologist, a blood specialist. And ask:

- Why is my hemoglobin low?
- Is it a lack of iron, or could it be something else?
- If I am short of iron, where is it going?
- Could I be a carrier of thalassemia or hemoglobin E?
- How long should I take the medicine, and when will you test my blood again?

# Common questions

## Which foods increase hemoglobin?

Fish, meat, eggs, dal and green leafy vegetables all give iron, and fruit with vitamin C, such as lemon or guava, helps the body take it in. Tea with meals does the opposite. But food cannot tell you why your hemoglobin fell, so see a doctor as well.

## Can I take iron tablets on my own for low hemoglobin?

It is better not to. Iron helps only when a lack of iron is the cause. Taken blindly, it can hide a problem or do nothing at all. And keep iron tablets out of children's reach, because too much iron is dangerous for a child.

## Is low hemoglobin dangerous?

Mild anaemia is common and can usually be put right. Severe anaemia, left alone, strains the heart, and in pregnancy it can affect both mother and baby. Often the cause matters even more than the number.
