If your blood report says your hemoglobin is low, you are in very large company. In West Bengal, the most recent national family health survey found anaemia in about 7 out of every 10 women who were not pregnant, about 6 out of every 10 pregnant women, and about 7 out of every 10 children under five. It is common enough that many people stop taking it seriously. That is a mistake, and this chapter explains why.

Anaemia is not one disease. It is a sign that something is wrong, the way a fever is a sign. Sometimes the reason is simple, like not enough iron in the food. Sometimes it is a family condition like thalassemia trait, and sometimes it is a warning of something that needs a closer look. The treatment depends entirely on the reason, so finding the reason is the real work.

# How blood normally carries oxygen

Your blood is full of red blood cells. Inside each one is a protein called hemoglobin. Think of hemoglobin as a lorry: it picks up oxygen in the lungs and drops it off at every part of the body, your brain, your heart, your muscles.

Red blood cells are made in the bone marrow, the soft tissue inside your bones. Each red cell lives for about three to four months and is then removed. So your marrow is working all the time, making fresh cells to replace the old ones. To do this it needs raw material, mainly iron, vitamin B12 and folic acid. When the kidneys sense that oxygen is running short, they send a hormone called erythropoietin to tell the marrow to make more.

# What anaemia means

Anaemia means there is less hemoglobin in your blood than there should be. Fewer lorries means less oxygen reaching your organs. That is why the main feelings of anaemia are tiredness and breathlessness.

Doctors decide whether hemoglobin is low by comparing it with the expected level for your age, sex and whether you are pregnant. The national survey in India counts anaemia as:

- below 12 g/dL in women who are not pregnant
- below 11 g/dL in pregnant women
- below 11 g/dL in children aged 6 months to 5 years

Men normally have a little more hemoglobin than women, so the cut off for men is a bit higher. Every laboratory prints its own normal range next to your result. Use that range, and let your doctor tell you what your number means for you.

# What causes it

Almost every cause of anaemia fits into one of three groups. The body is not making enough red cells, the body is losing blood, or red cells are being destroyed too early.

## Not making enough

- Not enough iron. This is the most common cause in the world and in India. It happens when food has too little iron, when the body cannot absorb it well, or when iron is being lost through bleeding.
- Not enough vitamin B12 or folic acid. Vitamin B12 comes mainly from animal foods, so people who eat no meat, fish, eggs or milk products are more at risk. Some people also cannot absorb B12 from the stomach.
- Long term illnesses such as kidney disease, rheumatoid arthritis, long infections like tuberculosis, and some cancers. These slow down red cell production.
- Problems in the bone marrow itself, such as aplastic anaemia, leukaemia, myeloma or myelodysplasia. These are less common, but they are the reason a hematologist sometimes needs to look deeper.

## Losing blood

- Heavy periods. A very common and often ignored cause in women and teenage girls.
- Pregnancy and childbirth, especially if there was heavy bleeding during or after delivery.
- Slow bleeding from the stomach or intestine. This can come from ulcers, piles, regular use of painkillers such as aspirin or ibuprofen, worms in the gut (hookworm), and less commonly a growth in the stomach or bowel. This kind of bleeding is often not visible.

## Red cells destroyed too early

- Inherited conditions such as thalassemia, hemoglobin E disease and sickle cell disease. Thalassemia trait and hemoglobin E are common in Bengal, and they are a frequent reason for small red cells on a report.
- The immune system attacking the red cells, certain infections such as malaria, and certain medicines. Doctors call this hemolysis. It can cause jaundice, the yellow colour of the eyes and skin.

# Who is most at risk

- Women in the years when they have periods, and pregnant women
- Babies, young children and teenage girls, because they are growing fast
- People whose diet is mostly rice or roti with little dal, green leaves, fish, meat or eggs
- People with long term illnesses, especially kidney disease
- People with a family history of thalassemia or other blood diseases
- Older people, in whom anaemia should always be explained, never just accepted as age

# What you may feel

Mild anaemia often causes no symptoms at all and is found only on a routine blood test. When it builds up slowly, the body gets used to it and people blame the tiredness on work, age or the heat.

Common symptoms are:

- tiredness and lack of energy, often worst by the afternoon
- getting breathless on stairs, or with work that used to be easy
- a pounding or racing heartbeat (palpitations)
- headaches and dizziness, especially on standing up
- looking pale, in the face, the inside of the eyelids, the palms and the nails
- cold hands and feet
- poor concentration, irritability, and in children, falling behind at school or not wanting to play

Some signs point more towards iron deficiency:

- craving to eat things that are not food, such as raw rice, mud, chalk, paper or ice
- a sore tongue, cracks at the corners of the mouth
- hair falling more than usual
- brittle nails, or nails that become flat or spoon shaped
- restless legs at night

Numbness or tingling in the hands and feet, difficulty walking in a straight line, or memory problems can be signs of vitamin B12 deficiency. These need treatment early, because some nerve problems from low B12 can become permanent.

# Go to a hospital now if

- you have chest pain or chest tightness
- you are breathless while sitting or lying down
- you faint, or nearly faint
- you are vomiting blood, or passing black, tar like stools, or blood in the stools
- you have very heavy bleeding that does not stop, from anywhere
- a pregnant woman with known anaemia becomes breathless, dizzy or very weak
- a child with anaemia becomes drowsy, breathless or stops feeding
- anaemia comes with high fever, yellow eyes and dark urine, or with new bruises or bleeding from the gums

# The tests

## Complete blood count (CBC)

This is the first test. A small sample of blood is taken from a vein in your arm. You do not need to be fasting. The report has many lines, but these are the ones that matter most for anaemia:

- Hemoglobin (Hb): the main number. It tells how much oxygen carrying protein your blood has.
- Red blood cell count (RBC): how many red cells there are.
- Hematocrit or PCV: how much of your blood is made of red cells.
- MCV: the average size of your red cells. This is a very useful clue. Small cells point towards iron deficiency or thalassemia trait. Large cells point towards vitamin B12 or folic acid deficiency, liver problems, thyroid problems or a marrow problem.
- MCH and MCHC: how much hemoglobin is packed in each cell.
- RDW: how much the cells vary in size.
- White cells and platelets: if these are also low or high, the marrow may need a closer look.

## Tests to find the reason

Depending on the CBC, your doctor may ask for:

- Serum ferritin and iron studies, to check your iron stores. Ferritin is a good test of stored iron, but infection or inflammation can push it up and hide a shortage of iron.
- Vitamin B12 and folate levels.
- Hemoglobin electrophoresis or HPLC, to look for thalassemia trait, hemoglobin E and other inherited hemoglobin conditions. This is especially useful in Bengal, where these are common.
- Peripheral smear: a doctor looks at your blood cells under a microscope.
- Reticulocyte count: how many young red cells your marrow is making. It tells whether the marrow is trying to keep up.
- Kidney, liver and thyroid tests.
- Stool test for hidden blood or worms.
- Endoscopy or colonoscopy, a camera test of the stomach or bowel, if bleeding from the gut is suspected. This matters most in men and in women after menopause who have iron deficiency, because in them the iron is usually being lost somewhere.
- Bone marrow test, only when the blood tests suggest the marrow itself is not working properly. It is covered in its own chapter.

# Treatment

The treatment of anaemia is the treatment of its cause. Raising the hemoglobin without finding out why it fell is like mopping the floor without fixing the leak.

## Iron

For iron deficiency, iron tablets or syrup are the usual first step. Your doctor will decide the kind of iron, the dose and how long to take it. A few things help:

- Iron is absorbed better on an empty stomach, but if it upsets your stomach, your doctor may suggest taking it with food instead.
- Take it with something containing vitamin C, such as a little lemon water or an amla, which helps absorption.
- Keep a gap between iron and tea, coffee, milk and calcium tablets, which block absorption.
- Black stools are normal on iron. Constipation, nausea and stomach pain are common side effects. Tell your doctor rather than stopping on your own, because there are other forms of iron that suit some people better.
- Hemoglobin usually starts rising within a few weeks, but the treatment continues for some months after the number becomes normal, to refill the body's iron stores. Stopping when you feel better is the most common reason anaemia comes back.
- Keep iron tablets away from children. Too much iron can seriously poison a small child.

When tablets do not work, cannot be tolerated, or the hemoglobin needs to rise faster (for example late in pregnancy or before surgery), iron can be given into a vein in a hospital or clinic.

A word of caution that matters in Bengal: small red cells are not always iron deficiency. Thalassemia trait also causes small red cells, and iron does not help it. Taking iron for months when you do not need it can do harm. This is why your doctor may check your ferritin, or test for thalassemia, before starting long treatment.

## Vitamin B12 and folic acid

These are replaced with tablets or injections. People who cannot absorb vitamin B12 from the stomach usually need injections, sometimes for life. Your doctor decides the form and how often.

## Treating the cause

- Heavy periods are treated, often with the help of a gynaecologist.
- Worms are treated with deworming medicine.
- Ulcers or bleeding in the gut are treated.
- Kidney disease, thyroid problems and long infections are treated by the right specialist, sometimes with an injection called erythropoietin that helps the marrow make red cells.
- Inherited conditions and marrow diseases are treated by a hematologist, and each has its own chapter on this site.

## Blood transfusion

A blood transfusion raises the hemoglobin quickly, but it does not treat the cause. It is used when anaemia is severe, when there are dangerous symptoms such as breathlessness at rest or chest pain, after heavy bleeding, and in some conditions like thalassemia major where regular blood is part of the treatment. Many people with iron deficiency, even with quite low hemoglobin, can be treated safely with iron alone if they are stable. Your doctor will weigh up the need for blood.

# Living with anaemia

## Food

Food alone rarely corrects established iron deficiency, but a good diet helps keep anaemia away once treated.

- Good sources of iron in a Bengali kitchen: fish, meat, liver, eggs, dal and other pulses, chickpeas (chhola), and dark green leafy vegetables (shak).
- Iron from fish, meat and eggs is absorbed more easily than iron from plants.
- Eat vegetables and fruits with vitamin C, such as lemon, guava, amla and oranges, with your meals.
- Drink tea and coffee between meals, not with them.
- If you eat no animal foods at all, talk to your doctor about vitamin B12.

## Pregnancy

Pregnancy needs a lot of extra iron, and anaemia in pregnancy affects both mother and baby. Take the iron and folic acid tablets your doctor or the government health centre gives you through the whole pregnancy, and get your hemoglobin checked as advised. If you have a family history of thalassemia, or small red cells on your report, ask about thalassemia testing, ideally for both partners and ideally before pregnancy.

## Children

Children with anaemia may be slow to learn, tired and more prone to infections. Many children need iron, deworming and a better diet together. Do not give a child adult iron tablets. Ask the doctor for the right form.

## Work, exercise and daily life

Most people with mild or moderate anaemia can keep working. Rest when you need to, and build up activity slowly as your hemoglobin rises. If you get chest pain, faintness or severe breathlessness with exertion, stop and see a doctor.

# Myths and facts

## "Beetroot and pomegranate will cure my anaemia"

They are healthy foods, but they contain much less iron than people think. Once you have iron deficiency anaemia, food alone usually cannot refill the stores. You need treatment of the cause and, usually, iron medicine.

## "Iron tablets make you fat" or "iron tablets heat the body"

Iron tablets do not cause weight gain. They can cause stomach upset and constipation, which your doctor can help with.

## "I feel fine now, so I can stop the iron"

You often feel better long before the body's iron stores are full. Stopping early is the commonest reason the anaemia returns.

## "Anaemia is normal for women"

Anaemia is common in women, but it is never normal. It causes tiredness, affects pregnancy, and can hide another illness.

## "Anaemia at my age is just old age"

Anaemia in an older person always needs a reason, because it can be the first sign of bleeding from the gut, kidney disease or a marrow problem.

# Questions to ask your doctor

- What type of anaemia do I have, and what is causing it?
- How low is my hemoglobin, and how quickly do you expect it to rise?
- Do I need any more tests to find the cause?
- Could I have thalassemia trait or hemoglobin E? Should my family be tested?
- How long do I need to take the medicine, and what side effects should I watch for?
- When will you check my blood again?
- Is there anything in my diet or my other medicines that I should change?
- What symptoms mean I should come back early?

# Common questions

## Is anaemia dangerous?

Mild anaemia is usually not dangerous in itself, but it should always be explained. Severe anaemia can strain the heart, and in pregnancy it raises risks for both mother and baby. The cause, such as hidden bleeding, can also be serious.

## How long does it take to recover from anaemia?

With iron deficiency, most people start to feel better in a few weeks and the hemoglobin rises steadily. Treatment usually continues for some months to refill the iron stores. Other types depend on their cause.

## Can anaemia be cured completely?

Anaemia due to iron, vitamin B12 or folic acid deficiency can usually be fully corrected once the cause is treated. Inherited types such as thalassemia major cannot be cured with tablets, but they can be managed well. Your doctor can tell you which type you have.

## Is anaemia the same as having low blood?

People often say "blood is low", but it is the hemoglobin that is low. The amount of blood is often normal. It is the oxygen carrying power that has fallen.

## What is the normal hemoglobin level?

It depends on age, sex and pregnancy. In India, anaemia is counted below 12 g/dL in women who are not pregnant and below 11 g/dL in pregnant women and in young children. Men normally run a little higher. Always check the range printed on your own report.

## Can tea cause anaemia?

Tea does not cause anaemia by itself, but drinking it with meals reduces the iron you absorb from food. Drinking tea between meals instead of with them is a simple habit that helps.

## Should I see a hematologist for anaemia?

Many cases are handled well by a family doctor or gynaecologist. See a hematologist if the cause is not clear, if the anaemia does not improve with treatment, if white cells or platelets are also abnormal, if there is a family history of blood disease, or if thalassemia is suspected.

# The main points

- Anaemia means low hemoglobin. It is a sign of a problem, not a final diagnosis.
- Iron deficiency is the most common cause, but in Bengal, thalassemia trait and hemoglobin E must also be thought of.
- Finding and treating the cause matters more than raising the number.
- Take iron for as long as your doctor advises, not just until you feel better.
- Chest pain, breathlessness at rest, fainting or black stools need a hospital the same day.
