> Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, CLL, is a slow-growing blood cancer of the lymphocytes that mostly affects older people and is often found on a blood report before it causes any trouble. It cannot usually be cured, but it can be controlled for years, and many people are first checked regularly and start treatment only when it is needed.

The blood test was only for the cataract operation, and now the eye doctor wanted him to see a hematologist.

He is sixty-six, a retired bank clerk in Salt Lake, who walks in the park every morning and feels perfectly well. His report showed a high white cell count, most of them lymphocytes. The eye doctor said the cataract could wait a little while a hematologist looked at the blood count.

In a town near Chinsurah, a widow of seventy has had swellings in her neck for months. This year she has had three chest infections, she wakes up with her blouse damp with sweat, and she is tired by noon. Her daughter, who lives in Kolkata, has taken leave to bring her to the city.

He wants to know why, if it is leukaemia, nobody is treating it. Her daughter wants to know whether her mother can be treated at her age.

# What CLL is

Lymphocytes are white blood cells that fight infection. In CLL, the bone marrow makes too many lymphocytes that cannot fight infection well. Over years they build up in the blood, the marrow and the glands (lymph nodes), and leave less room for healthy cells.

CLL usually grows very slowly. It is more common as people get older, and very rare under forty. In the beginning there are often no signs at all, and it is found on a blood test done for something else, as it was for him.

# Why nobody is treating him yet

After the diagnosis, more tests show how far the CLL has developed. These can include scans, a chest X-ray, and blood and bone marrow tests.

Many people with CLL do not need treatment straight away. Instead, the hematologist checks them regularly, with blood tests and an examination. This is called watch and wait. It is a plan, with check-ups, and treatment starts when signs or tests show it is needed.

Between check-ups, new signs are reported to the hematologist without waiting for the next appointment.

# Why her infections mattered

Because the CLL cells cannot fight infection well, people with CLL can get ill more often. Repeated infections, swollen glands, night sweats and tiredness together can mean that CLL needs treatment.

# Signs of CLL

Many people have no signs. When they come, they can include:

- swollen glands (lymph nodes), usually in the neck or under the arms
- getting ill a lot
- feeling tired even after a good night's sleep
- losing weight
- sweating at night, or a fever without feeling unwell
- bleeding or bruising for no reason, or a rash like small bruises that does not fade when a glass is pressed on it
- looking pale and feeling breathless

If the blood report does not show a cause, see a hematologist if you have these signs.

A gland (lymph node) that has not gone down after two weeks, or keeps growing, needs a doctor. Left for months, the cause goes unchecked, and if it is a cancer, finding it early can make it more treatable.

# When treatment starts, and what it does

When to start depends on the signs, how far the CLL has developed and the person's general health. The main treatments are targeted medicines. These include newer tablets such as ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib, pirtobrutinib, venetoclax, idelalisib and duvelisib, sometimes given with antibody medicines such as obinutuzumab, rituximab or ofatumumab. For some people whose CLL comes back, CAR T-cell therapy may be used. Chemotherapy is used less often. Rarely, radiotherapy or an operation to remove the spleen is needed.

Treatment cannot usually cure CLL, but it can control it, and this control can last for years. CLL can come back, and treatment may be repeated several times over the years. People also get help to prevent infections, including medicines, vaccines the hematologist advises and, when needed, blood or platelet transfusions.

# How the stories go on

The retired clerk has his cataract operation. He sees the hematologist regularly, keeps his reports in a folder, and keeps walking in the park. He learns which signs to report between visits.

The widow's tests show CLL that needs treatment. Her hematologist starts targeted medicine and plans how to prevent infections. Her daughter learns which signs mean the hospital, and a neighbour checks on her mother every evening.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- a high fever or shivering
- bleeding that does not stop, or a rash like small bruises that does not fade under a glass
- very pale, or breathless at rest
- swollen glands with difficulty breathing or swallowing

# Take these questions to your doctor

A hematologist leads the care of CLL. Bring your reports and ask:

- Do I need treatment now, or regular check-ups first?
- How often will I be checked, and what will be tested?
- Which signs should I report between visits?
- If treatment is needed, what will it aim for?
- How can I lower the chance of infections, and which vaccines should I have?
- Can I have other operations, like a cataract operation, as planned?

# Common questions

## Is CLL curable?

It cannot usually be cured, but treatment can control it, often for years. Many people live with CLL for a long time, and treatment may be repeated when it is needed.

## Why is CLL not treated straight away?

CLL usually grows very slowly, and many people have no signs for a long time. The hematologist checks regularly and starts treatment when signs or tests show it is needed.

## Is CLL the same as acute leukaemia?

No. Acute leukaemia grows over weeks and usually needs treatment at once. CLL usually grows very slowly.
