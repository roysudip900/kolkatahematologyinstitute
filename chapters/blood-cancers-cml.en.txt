> Chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML) is a slow blood cancer in which the bone marrow makes too many white cells, and it is usually treatable with targeted tablets such as imatinib. The tablets work only when taken without gaps and checked with regular tests, so never stop or change them on your own, and tell your hematologist about any side effect.

The office check-up was meant to be a formality.

He is forty-five, an accountant, and he felt fine. The report came back with a white cell count far above the range, and a note asking him to see a hematologist. That evening he remembered that for a few months he had felt full after half a plate of rice, and had put it down to gas. He did not sleep.

In another part of the city, a woman of thirty-eight has been taking a tablet for CML for three years. She feels completely well. Her last tests were good, the tablets are expensive, and her sister said she was cured. Two months ago she stopped them without telling anyone.

He wants to know what this means for his life. She wants to know why she should keep taking a tablet when she is well.

# What CML is

The bone marrow, the soft tissue inside the bones, makes all the blood cells. In CML, a change in the genes of one blood-making cell, called the Philadelphia chromosome, makes the marrow produce far too many white cells. This change happens during a person's life. It is not passed from parent to child.

CML usually grows slowly, over years. Doctors describe it in three phases: the chronic phase, which is slow and steady, then the accelerated phase and the blast phase, in which immature cells crowd the marrow and the illness behaves more like an acute leukaemia. Finding it early, in the chronic phase, may make it easier to treat.

His feeling of fullness came from the spleen, an organ under the left ribs, which swells as it fills with the extra cells.

# The tablets

The main treatment for CML is a group of targeted tablets that block the faulty signal driving the marrow. Imatinib was the first of them, and there are now several others. Many people on these tablets go on working and living at home, with regular visits to their hematologist.

The tablets need to be taken regularly, without gaps, exactly as the hematologist says. If they cause side effects, tell the doctor instead of skipping doses. A different tablet can often be tried. If cost is a problem, say so too. Some people need chemotherapy or a stem cell transplant, usually when the tablets stop working or the illness has reached a later phase.

# The tests that keep watch

Regular blood tests, and a genetic test that measures the faulty gene in the blood, show how well the tablets are working. These results guide every decision: whether to continue, change or, for some people with very good results over years, try stopping under the hematologist's close watch, with the tests going on.

The woman felt well because the tablets were working, and only the tests could show whether the CML was still held back. Stopping them on her own, without the tests, gave the CML a chance to grow back unnoticed.

# Signs that should not wait

CML often causes no symptoms early, and it is sometimes found on a blood test done for another reason. When there are signs, they include tiredness, easy bruising or bleeding, frequent infections, fullness or pain under the left ribs, weight loss, night sweats, bone pain, and swollen glands in the neck, armpit or groin.

If the blood report does not show a cause, see a hematologist if you have these signs.

A swelling in the belly or a gland that stays for weeks needs a doctor's look. Left for months, CML can move from the slow phase to the faster ones, which are harder to treat.

# How the stories go on

The accountant's genetic test confirms CML in the chronic phase. He starts the tablet, keeps a small box of them in his office drawer, and goes back for his tests on every date he is given. After some weeks the fullness after meals is gone.

The woman tells her hematologist the truth at her next visit. Her tests show the CML has started to come back. She restarts treatment, the hematologist talks with her about the cost, and she now keeps every test date.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- bleeding that does not stop, or blood in vomit or stools
- a high fever with shivering, or feeling very unwell
- sudden severe pain under the left ribs
- an erection that does not go down after a long time

# Take these questions to your doctor

A hematologist looks after CML for many years. Ask:

- Which phase is my CML in?
- What do my tests show, and when are the next ones?
- What should I do about this side effect?
- What happens if I miss a tablet?
- Could I ever stop treatment, and how would that be done safely?
- If the tablets are hard to afford, what can we do?

# Common questions

## Is CML curable?

CML is usually treatable, and the targeted tablets can keep it in its slow phase for many years. A stem cell transplant is used for some people, usually when the tablets stop working or the illness has reached a later phase.

## Can I stop imatinib if I feel fine?

No, not on your own. Feeling fine is a sign the tablets are working. Only your hematologist can decide on stopping, based on years of test results, and the tests continue afterwards.

## Is CML passed on to children?

No. The gene change in CML happens during a person's life and is not passed from parent to child.
