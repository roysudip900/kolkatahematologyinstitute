> Chemotherapy for blood cancers often lowers the white cells that fight infection, so an infection can turn serious fast. A fever, chills or feeling suddenly unwell during treatment needs a call to the hospital at once, and clean hands, safe food and careful visitors help keep infections away.

He thought the fever could wait until morning.

He is forty, a schoolteacher from Bankura, and for his chemotherapy he and his wife have rented a small room near the hospital in Kolkata. Some days after a treatment, at midnight, he felt cold and began to shiver. He did not want to wake the doctor or spend on a taxi, so he lay under two blankets and told his wife it was only the weather.

In a house in Hooghly, a girl of twelve is on long treatment for leukaemia at home. On Sunday the relatives come with sweets, the house is full, and a cousin with a running nose wants to sit next to her. Her mother does not know how to say no.

He wants to know why one fever is such a fuss. Her mother wants to know how to keep her daughter safe without shutting her away.

# Why white cells matter during treatment

White cells fight infection, and the main ones are called neutrophils. Some blood cancers, and treatments such as chemotherapy, lower the number of these cells. During each round of chemotherapy there are times when they are especially low, and the risk of infection is highest.

Your blood tests show when the count is low. Sometimes the hematologist gives medicine to prevent infection, or to help the marrow make white cells.

# Why a fever cannot wait until morning

An infection during cancer treatment can be life-threatening and needs urgent care. It can lead to sepsis, a serious reaction to an infection that develops very quickly and needs urgent treatment in hospital.

The hospital tells each family what temperature to act on. The National Cancer Institute gives 38 °C (100.5 °F) or higher. Keep a thermometer at home and use it. Ask the team before taking any medicine for a fever, even paracetamol.

Signs of an infection can also be:

- chills or shivering
- a cough or sore throat
- loose motions
- a sore mouth, or white patches on the tongue
- burning or pain when passing urine
- redness, swelling or pain around a line or catheter
- a sore or bleeding bottom

# Keeping germs away at home

- Wash your hands often with soap and water, especially before eating, and ask everyone around you to do the same.
- Clean your teeth gently every day and check your mouth for sores.
- Clean any cut or scrape well, and keep any line or catheter clean and dry.
- Eat food that is well cooked, especially meat, fish and eggs. Keep hot food hot and cold food cold. You may be advised to eat only fruit and vegetables that can be peeled, or to wash all raw fruit and vegetables very well.
- Stay away from crowds and from people who have a cold, a cough or a fever.
- Ask the team about anyone at home who has just had a live vaccine, such as for chicken pox.

Visitors who are well, who wash their hands and who come a few at a time are a comfort. Anyone with a cold, cough, fever or loose motions can phone instead, or come once they are well.

# How the stories go on

At two in the morning his wife calls the number on his hospital card. The nurse tells them to come in at once. He gets antibiotics through a drip, and the fever settles over the next days. Now the thermometer and the hospital number sit next to his bed.

The girl's mother asks the hematologist, who explains which visitors are safe. On Sundays the cousins now wave from the doorway when they have a cold, and everyone washes their hands at the tap before they sit with her.

# Go to a hospital now if

During treatment, call the hospital at once and go in if there is:

- a temperature at or above the level your hospital gave you, or shivering you cannot stop
- a temperature that is very low, or feeling suddenly very unwell even with no fever
- difficulty breathing, or breathing very fast
- confusion or slurred speech
- not passing urine all day, or very little
- blue, pale, grey or blotchy skin, lips or tongue

# Take these questions to your doctor

Ask the hematologist or the nurse:

- When in my treatment am I most at risk of infection?
- What temperature should I act on, and whom do I call, day or night?
- Should I take anything for a fever, or call first?
- What food should I avoid while my count is low?
- Who can visit, and when?
- Will I get medicine to prevent infection or to raise my white cells?

# Common questions

## Is one fever during chemotherapy really an emergency?

Yes. During treatment an infection can become life-threatening and needs urgent care. Call the hospital at once, day or night.

## Can I eat outside food during treatment?

Ask your team. Meat, fish and eggs should be well cooked, and raw fruit and vegetables peeled or washed very well.

## Should family stop visiting?

No, but visitors should be well and wash their hands. Anyone with a cold, cough or fever can phone instead.
