> Acute leukaemia is a fast-growing blood cancer in which immature cells fill the bone marrow within weeks, and its two main types are AML and ALL. It is often treatable, but treatment usually needs to start quickly, so tiredness, paleness, bruising, bleeding or fevers that keep coming need a blood count and a hematologist without delay.

The wedding was five weeks away, and she wanted the blood test to wait until after.

She is fifty-two and teaches in a school in Howrah. For three weeks she had been tired in a way she had never known, her gums bled when she brushed, and there were small red spots on her legs. Her family doctor checked her blood and asked her to see a hematologist the next day. Her daughter's wedding cards had already gone out, the caterer was booked, and she thought a few weeks would make no difference.

Across the river, a boy of fifteen has had pain in his legs for a month. His mother called it growing pains, his coach called it a football strain. Then he became pale, tired after climbing the stairs, and bruises appeared that he could not explain. His board exams are in March.

She wants to know whether the hospital can wait. His parents want to know whether he will be able to study.

# What acute leukaemia is

The bone marrow, the soft tissue inside the bones, makes red cells, white cells and platelets. In acute leukaemia, young blood cells that have not grown up properly, called blasts, multiply fast and fill the marrow. With too few healthy cells, people become tired and pale, they get infections, and they bruise and bleed easily.

There are two main types. Acute myeloid leukaemia, AML, can happen at any age but is more common in older people. Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, ALL, is most common in children and young people. Both usually develop over a few weeks.

# Why the wedding cannot come first

Acute leukaemia usually needs treatment straight away, because it grows over weeks. Waiting a month can let the marrow fill further, and the infections and bleeding become harder to manage.

Her hematologist explains that the family can still have the wedding, but she will start treatment now and may watch part of it from a hospital bed or a phone screen. The specialists plan treatment around the family as much as they can.

# Why his leg pain mattered

Pain in the bones or joints is one of the signs of acute leukaemia, because the marrow inside the bones is crowded. Most leg pain in a teenager comes from growing or sport. But leg pain with paleness, tiredness and unexplained bruises needs a blood count the same week.

# Signs that should not wait

The signs of acute leukaemia usually appear over a few weeks:

- feeling very tired or weak, and looking pale
- bruising or bleeding for no reason, from the gums, the nose or under the skin
- getting ill a lot, fevers, or taking longer than usual to recover
- pain in the bones or joints
- swollen glands (lymph nodes) in the neck, armpits or groin, or a swollen tummy
- breathlessness, or losing weight without trying

If the blood report does not show a cause, see a hematologist if you have these signs.

Swollen glands or a swollen tummy that do not settle within a couple of weeks need a doctor's look. In acute leukaemia even a few weeks of waiting matters, because the illness grows fast and can become harder to treat.

# What treatment asks of a family

Acute leukaemia is often treatable. In children with ALL, where full treatment is available, most children who complete it survive. In AML, the outlook depends on the type, the person's age and their general health.

Treatment is long and asks a great deal. It is usually chemotherapy, often given in hospital in several phases over months, and for ALL it includes steroids, taken only as the hematologist plans. Some people also need targeted medicines, immunotherapy, radiotherapy or a stem cell or bone marrow transplant. For some older people with AML, gentler treatment at home aims to control the leukaemia for as long as possible.

Alongside it come antibiotics to prevent and treat infections, blood transfusions, and medicines that help the marrow recover. During treatment the body fights infection poorly, so any fever needs a call to the hospital at once. Families learn to keep a thermometer, a notebook and the hospital's number close.

# How the stories go on

The teacher starts treatment the same week. She watches her daughter's wedding on a video call from the ward, in a new sari her sister brought in. Months of treatment follow, and she keeps every appointment.

The boy's blood count leads to a bone marrow test, and he is found to have ALL. His treatment begins in hospital. His school lets him sit his exams later. His mother keeps a notebook of every temperature, and the coach visits with a football signed by the team.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- a high fever or shivering, above all during treatment
- bleeding that does not stop, or blood in vomit, urine or stools
- very pale, drowsy, or breathless at rest
- a face, neck or arms that suddenly swell and turn red, or swollen veins in the neck or chest
- a fit, or a sudden severe headache

# Take these questions to your doctor

A hematologist leads the care of acute leukaemia. Ask:

- Is this AML or ALL, and which type exactly?
- How soon must treatment start, and where?
- What does treatment aim for in my case?
- What should we do when a fever comes, day or night?
- How long will treatment take, and how will it affect work or school?
- Is a transplant likely to be part of the plan?

# Common questions

## Is acute leukaemia curable?

It is often treatable. In children with ALL, where full treatment is available, most who complete it survive. In AML, the outlook depends on the type, age and general health, and the hematologist can explain what treatment aims for.

## How quickly does acute leukaemia need treatment?

Usually straight away. It grows over weeks, so treatment normally starts as soon as the diagnosis is made.

## Can adults get ALL?

Yes. ALL is most common in children and young people, but adults can get it too, and AML is more common in older people.
