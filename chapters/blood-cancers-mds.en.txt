> Myelodysplastic syndrome, MDS, is a rare blood cancer, mostly of older people, in which the bone marrow makes blood cells that do not grow up properly, so the blood counts fall. It often develops slowly and cannot usually be cured, but treatment can often manage it, so tiredness, bruising or infections that keep coming need a hematologist.

He thought the tiredness was just his age, and kept buying tonics from the chemist.

He is seventy-four, a retired railway guard in Kharagpur. For two years he has been breathless on the stairs, and his wife says he looks pale. When his hemoglobin fell again, the iron tablets made no difference, and the doctor sent him to a hematologist. Now he needs blood transfusions, and he hates the long ride to the hospital.

In Bally, a woman of sixty-eight had a blood test for her sugar check-up. Her white cells and platelets were a little low. She felt well, and her son wanted to know why the doctor was talking about the bone marrow.

He wants to know why iron and tonics did not work. Her son wants to know whether his mother has cancer.

# What MDS is

The bone marrow, the soft tissue inside the bones, makes red cells, white cells and platelets. In MDS, young blood cells in the marrow do not grow up into healthy cells, so the blood is short of healthy cells: too few red cells, too few white cells, too few platelets, or all three.

There are several types of MDS, depending on which blood cells are affected and how. MDS often develops slowly, over several years. Some people with MDS go on to develop acute myeloid leukaemia, AML. It is more common in people over seventy and in men. In most people the cause is not known. Rarely, it follows chemotherapy or radiotherapy for another cancer.

# Why iron and tonics did not help him

Iron helps when the body is short of iron. In MDS, the problem is in the marrow, so iron or tonics cannot fix it. Do not start or stop tonics, iron or any other medicine because of a blood report. Show the report to the hematologist first.

The main tests for MDS are blood tests and a bone marrow test, in which a small sample is taken from the hip bone with the skin numbed. Genetic tests on the marrow show the type of MDS and the risk group, which means how fast it is likely to get worse and how likely it is to turn into AML.

# Why she was checked though she felt well

Many people have no signs at first, and MDS is found on a blood test done for something else, as it was for her. Signs usually come slowly. They can include:

- feeling tired or weak
- feeling breathless with light work, like climbing the stairs
- bleeding or bruising more than usual, such as nosebleeds, bleeding gums or heavier periods
- getting ill a lot, or taking longer to recover
- looking paler than usual
- a rash like small bruises that does not fade when a glass is pressed on it

If the blood report does not show a cause, see a hematologist if you have these signs.

# What treatment can do

Treatment depends on the type of MDS, the risk group, the signs, age and general health.

People with low-risk MDS and few signs usually have regular check-ups and blood tests first, and start treatment if it gets worse. Many people also need supportive treatment: blood transfusions, medicines called growth factors that help the marrow make more blood cells, and antibiotics for infections. People who need many transfusions may need chelation, a medicine that removes the extra iron.

Other treatments are used for some types of MDS. These include azacitidine and decitabine, which kill fast-dividing cells and may slow the change to AML, and newer medicines. Lenalidomide, luspatercept and imetelstat are used for anaemia in some people who need regular transfusions, and ivosidenib for some MDS that has come back or not responded. Higher-risk MDS may need chemotherapy and a stem cell transplant, which can sometimes cure MDS. A transplant needs healthy stem cells from a donor and carries risks, such as infections and the donor cells attacking the body (graft versus host disease), so it is not suitable for everyone.

MDS cannot usually be cured, and advanced MDS can be very hard to treat. Then treatment aims to control it and its symptoms, and help the person live longer.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- a high fever or shivering
- bleeding that does not stop, or blood in vomit, urine or stools
- very pale, drowsy, or breathless at rest
- a rash like small bruises that does not fade under a glass

# Take these questions to your doctor

A hematologist leads the care of MDS. Bring your reports and ask:

- Which type of MDS is it, and which risk group?
- Do I need treatment now, or regular check-ups first?
- What will treatment aim for in my case?
- How often might I need a transfusion, and what about the extra iron?
- Which signs should I report between visits?
- Is a stem cell transplant possible for me?

# Common questions

## Is MDS a cancer?

Yes, it is a rare type of blood cancer of the bone marrow. It often develops slowly, over several years.

## Can MDS be cured?

It cannot usually be cured. A stem cell transplant can sometimes cure it, but it needs a donor and carries risks, so it is not suitable for everyone. Other treatments can often manage the symptoms.

## Will iron tablets help?

Not for MDS itself, because the problem is in the marrow. Do not start or stop iron or tonics on your own. The hematologist will decide what you need.
