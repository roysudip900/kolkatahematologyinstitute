> Multiple myeloma is a blood cancer of the plasma cells in the bone marrow that can weaken the bones, raise the calcium in the blood, harm the kidneys and lower the blood counts. It usually cannot be cured but can be managed, so worsening back or bone pain needs a doctor, and sudden confusion or weakness of the legs needs a hospital the same day.

His rib cracked when he coughed.

He is sixty-two, a retired bank clerk who still walks to the market every morning. It was a bad winter cough, and one evening he felt something give way in his side. The X-ray showed a broken rib. For months before that he had a dull ache in his back, and he had been tired in a way that sleep did not fix. He had been buying painkillers from the chemist.

In another home, a woman of sixty-seven has been thirsty all week, passing urine again and again, and her bowels have stopped. Her daughter came home to find her mother muddled, not sure what day it was, and vomiting. At the hospital, the calcium in her blood is very high and her kidneys are under strain.

He wants to know why a cough could break a bone. Her daughter wants to know what is happening to her mother.

# What myeloma is

Inside the bone marrow, a type of white cell called the plasma cell makes antibodies, the proteins that fight infection. In myeloma, abnormal plasma cells build up in the marrow. They crowd out the cells that make blood, they eat away at the bone from inside, and they make a protein the body does not need, which can harm the kidneys.

So myeloma can show itself in four ways: in the bones, in the calcium in the blood, in the kidneys, and in the blood counts.

# Why the rib broke

Myeloma cells weaken the bones, so a bone can break after a small strain, such as a cough or a light fall. The pain is often in the back or the ribs, and it is a dull, lasting ache that painkillers help less and less.

Back pain is common, and most of the time it comes from the muscles or the spine. But back or bone pain that keeps getting worse, especially with tiredness, needs a doctor. Tell the doctor which painkillers you have been taking.

# Why she became confused

When myeloma eats into bone, calcium from the bone floods into the blood. Too much calcium can cause thirst, passing urine often, constipation, loss of appetite, nausea or vomiting, weakness, restlessness and confusion. It can affect the kidneys, the nerves and the heart.

This is why sudden confusion, with thirst and vomiting, in an older person needs a hospital the same day.

# Signs of myeloma

Sometimes myeloma causes no signs and is found on a blood or urine test done for another reason. When there are signs, they include:

- bone pain, especially in the back, hips or ribs, or a bone that breaks easily
- tiredness that rest does not fix, and breathlessness
- frequent infections, or fever for no clear reason
- easy bruising or bleeding
- great thirst and passing urine often
- weight loss without trying

If the blood report does not show a cause, see a hematologist if you have these signs.

Bone or back pain that lasts for weeks and keeps getting worse needs a doctor's look. Left for months, myeloma can break bones, harm the kidneys and become harder to treat.

# What treatment can do

Myeloma usually cannot be cured, but it can be managed with treatment, and the plan is made for each person by a team of specialists. The kinds of treatment include:

- chemotherapy and targeted medicines, often given together
- steroids, which help the other medicines work, taken only as the hematologist plans
- medicines that slow bone loss and ease bone pain
- radiotherapy for a painful spot in a bone
- a stem cell transplant for some people in good general health

Newer drugs used in myeloma include thalidomide, lenalidomide, pomalidomide, bortezomib, carfilzomib, ixazomib, daratumumab and isatuximab, and for some people a newer treatment called CAR T-cell therapy. Which ones, and in what order, is the hematologist's decision.

Myeloma can come back after treatment, and then it is treated again. Regular blood and urine tests show how it is responding. Do not start, stop or change any of these medicines on your own, and ask the hematologist before taking any painkiller, calcium or vitamin tablet.

# How the stories go on

The retired clerk's blood and urine tests and a bone marrow test confirm myeloma. His treatment starts, medicine to protect his bones is added, and the back pain eases over the weeks. He still walks to the market, more slowly, and keeps every test date.

The woman's calcium is brought down in hospital and her kidneys recover partly. Myeloma is found, and treatment begins once she is stronger. Her daughter now keeps her mother's reports in one folder, and knows which signs mean the hospital.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- sudden numbness or weakness in the legs, being unable to walk or stand, or losing control of urine or stools
- sudden confusion, especially with thirst, vomiting or constipation
- difficulty breathing
- a high fever with shivering, in anyone with myeloma
- passing very little urine

# Take these questions to your doctor

A hematologist looks after myeloma for many years. Ask:

- Is this myeloma, and how far has it reached the bones and kidneys?
- What is my calcium, and are my kidneys working well?
- What is the treatment plan, and could a transplant be part of it?
- Which painkillers are safe for me?
- How will we know if the myeloma comes back?
- What should my family watch for at home?

# Common questions

## Is multiple myeloma curable?

It usually cannot be cured, but it can be managed with treatment. Myeloma can come back, and it can be treated again.

## Why does myeloma cause back pain?

Myeloma cells weaken the bones from inside, often in the spine and ribs. The pain is usually a dull ache that gets worse over time, and a bone can break after a small strain.

## Why is calcium high in myeloma?

As myeloma damages the bone, calcium from the bone passes into the blood. High calcium can cause thirst, constipation, vomiting and confusion, and needs treatment in hospital.
