> Blood cancers start in the blood-making cells of the bone marrow or lymph glands, and their early signs, such as tiredness, bruising, repeated fevers, lumps or bone pain, look like everyday illnesses. When such signs do not go away, ask your doctor for a blood count and see a hematologist, because many blood cancers can be treated and some can be cured.

He is eight, and he used to be first out of the door for football. Now he lies on the sofa after school. He is pale, there are bruises on his legs that he cannot explain, and his gums bled twice this week when he brushed. The local chemist suggested a tonic for weakness. His grandmother said it was the change of season. Then one evening his mother saw how white his lips were, and she was frightened.

In a flat in another part of the city, a retired clerk of sixty-six has had back pain for months. He thought he had strained it lifting a gas cylinder. The painkillers help less each week. He is tired all the time, always thirsty, and his trousers have become loose. His son thinks it is age. Nobody has asked for a blood test.

The mother wants to know whether her son will live. The clerk wants to know whether his pain is only his back.

# What a blood cancer is

Blood is made in the bone marrow, the soft tissue inside the bones, which makes red cells, white cells and platelets every day. Some white cells also live in the lymph glands (lymph nodes) of the neck, armpits and groin.

A blood cancer starts when one of these blood-making cells changes and starts to multiply out of control. The faulty cells crowd out the healthy ones. With too few red cells you become tired and pale. With too few working white cells, infections and fevers keep coming. With too few platelets, you bruise and bleed easily.

There are three main kinds. Leukaemia starts in the bone marrow and spreads into the blood. Lymphoma starts in the lymph glands. Myeloma starts in a type of white cell inside the bone marrow and often weakens the bones. Some kinds grow fast, over weeks, and some grow slowly, over years.

# Why the boy needs a blood count now

Fast-growing leukaemias usually show themselves over a few weeks: tiredness, paleness, bruising or bleeding for no reason, one illness after another, fever, pain in the bones or joints, a swollen tummy, or swollen glands. Each of these on its own has many common causes. Together, and not going away, they call for a blood count, which is quick and simple.

The tonic and the change of season were never going to explain bruises and bleeding gums. A blood count is simple, and it shows whether the bone marrow is struggling.

# When back pain needs a blood test

Myeloma often comes on gradually, over months. It can cause bone pain in the back, hips, shoulders or ribs, tiredness for no reason, breathlessness, great thirst, passing urine more often, and weight loss without trying. Sometimes it causes no symptoms and is found on a blood test done for another reason.

Back pain is common and usually comes from the muscles or the spine. But back pain that keeps getting worse, with tiredness, thirst and weight loss, needs a blood test. Tell the doctor how long the pain has lasted and which painkillers you have been taking.

# Signs that should lead to a blood test

Any of these, if they do not go away:

- tiredness and paleness that rest does not fix
- bruising or bleeding for no reason, from the gums, the nose or under the skin
- fevers and infections that keep coming back
- a painless lump in the neck, armpit or groin
- drenching sweats at night, or weight loss without trying
- bone pain that keeps getting worse

These signs are common, and most of the time the cause is not cancer. A blood count is the first step, and a doctor reads it together with how you are.

If the blood report does not show a cause, see a hematologist if you have these signs.

Glands (lymph nodes) in the neck, armpit or groin that swell with an infection usually go down within a week or two. A gland that is not going down, is growing, feels hard or does not move needs a doctor's look. Most often it is not cancer. Left for months, if the cause is lymphoma or another cancer, it can spread and become harder to treat.

# What treatment can do

Each blood cancer is different, and how serious it is depends on the type, the person's age and general health. Many can be treated, and some can be cured:

- Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, the most common cancer in children, now has very good results, and where full treatment is available, most children who complete it survive.
- Hodgkin lymphoma, if caught early, can often be removed completely by treatment, though it can come back.
- Some types, such as chronic lymphocytic leukaemia and myeloma, usually cannot be cured, but treatment can control them, often for years.

The kinds of treatment include chemotherapy, targeted tablets, radiation and a stem cell or bone marrow transplant. The plan is made by a team of specialists for each person. Each main type will have its own part in this chapter.

# How the stories go on

The boy's blood count is very abnormal, and he is sent to a hematologist the same day. A bone marrow test confirms acute lymphoblastic leukaemia. His treatment is long and hard on the whole family, with many months of hospital visits. His mother keeps a notebook of every fever, every instruction from the nurses, and every question.

The clerk's blood test leads to more tests, and he is found to have myeloma. It cannot be cured, but his treatment is started, his bones are protected, and the pain eases. His son now comes with him to every visit.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- bleeding that does not stop, or a child who is very pale, drowsy or breathless
- a high fever with shivering in anyone being treated for a blood cancer
- sudden numbness or weakness in the legs, being unable to walk or stand, or losing control of urine or stools
- sudden confusion
- a face, neck or arms that suddenly swell and turn red, or swollen veins in the neck or chest

# Take these questions to your doctor

If signs like these have not gone away, see your doctor, and see a hematologist if the blood count is abnormal. Ask:

- Can we do a blood count now?
- Could these signs come from the blood or the bone marrow?
- What other tests are needed, and how soon?
- If it is a blood cancer, which type is it, and how fast does it grow?
- What can treatment do for this type?
- Where should treatment happen, and what will it ask of our family?

# Common questions

## Can blood cancer be cured?

Some can. Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia in children and early Hodgkin lymphoma often respond very well. Some types usually cannot be cured, but treatment can control them for years.

## Does an abnormal blood count mean cancer?

Usually not. Many common, often minor, things change the blood count. The doctor reads the whole report together with how you are, and decides whether more tests are needed.

## Can a blood test find blood cancer?

A blood count is the first step and often shows that something is wrong. Confirming the type usually needs more tests, such as a bone marrow test or a lump biopsy.
