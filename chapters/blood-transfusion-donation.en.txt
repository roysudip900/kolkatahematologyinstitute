# Why blood donors are needed

Blood is needed by women with complications in pregnancy and childbirth, children with severe anaemia, people hurt in accidents, and people having operations or cancer treatment. Blood can be stored only for a limited time, so a regular supply from healthy donors is needed for it to be there when a patient needs it.

One donation can help more than one patient, because the blood is separated into red cells, platelets and plasma. Blood banks screen donated blood carefully to keep it safe.

The World Health Organization says regular voluntary donors who are not paid are the safest donors, because infections in the blood are least common among them. It asks every country to collect all its blood from such donors.

# Who can give blood

Most people who are healthy, and do not have an infection that can pass through the blood, can give blood. The age limits differ from country to country. The blood centre tells you the age, weight and how often you can give blood.

## Do not give blood for your own sake if

- you are feeling unwell
- you have anaemia
- you are pregnant, have been pregnant in the last year, or are breastfeeding
- you have certain illnesses, or take certain medicines, such as antibiotics

Many people in this list can give blood later.

## Do not give blood for the patient's sake if

- you have, or may recently have caught, an infection such as HIV, hepatitis or syphilis
- you have ever injected drugs that were not prescribed
- you recently had a tattoo, a piercing or skin marking, until the time the blood centre tells you has passed
- your lifestyle, or your partner's, puts you at risk of an infection passed through the blood

Be honest when you are asked. Blood is tested, but a test may not find a very recent infection, so the blood could still infect a patient. What you say is kept confidential.

# What happens when you give blood

- You answer questions about your health, your medicines, your lifestyle and recent travel.
- A short check: your pulse and blood pressure, your weight, and a drop of blood from your fingertip to make sure giving blood will not make you anaemic.
- You sit in a special chair or lie on a bed. The inside of your elbow is cleaned, and a trained health worker puts in a sterile needle joined to a blood bag.
- Giving blood usually takes about ten minutes.
- You rest for a short while and have a drink and a snack before you leave.

# Is giving blood safe?

Yes. You are accepted as a donor only if you are fit and well, and your blood is checked so that giving will not harm you. The needle and bag come in a sterile pack and are never used again, which makes giving blood as safe as possible.

You feel a gentle pressure and a quick pinprick. Discomfort or problems during or after giving blood are very uncommon.

Less than a tenth of your blood is taken. Your body replaces the lost fluid within about a day and a half.

## After giving blood

- Avoid hard work or heavy exercise for the rest of the day.
- Drink plenty of water over the next day.
- If you feel faint or dizzy, lie down or sit with your head low, and tell the staff.

# Go to a hospital now if

Go at once, or tell the blood centre staff straight away, if after giving blood you:

- faint and do not come round quickly, or keep feeling faint after lying down
- have chest pain or difficulty breathing
- have bleeding from the needle site that does not stop when you press on it
- have an arm that becomes very painful, swollen or numb

# Take these questions to the blood centre

- Can I give blood today, and if not, when can I come back?
- What are the age and weight limits here?
- How often can I give blood?
- I take a medicine. Can I still give blood?
- What should I do if I feel unwell after giving blood?

# Common questions

## Will giving blood make me weak?

It should not. You give blood only if the checks show you are well, and less than a tenth of your blood is taken. Your body replaces the fluid within about a day and a half. Avoid hard work for the rest of the day.

## Can I catch an infection from giving blood?

The needle and bag come in a sterile pack and are used only once, which makes giving blood as safe as possible.

## Why do they ask such personal questions?

To protect you and the patient. Tests may not find a very recent infection, so honest answers keep the blood safe. What you say is kept confidential.
