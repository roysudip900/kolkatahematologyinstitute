> A blood transfusion gives you donated red cells, platelets or plasma through a thin tube in a vein, when blood has been lost or the body cannot make enough. It is not a better or quicker treatment for anaemia: it manages an emergency, and the cause of the low haemoglobin still needs to be found and treated. It is generally very safe because the blood is tested and matched, but tell the nurse at once if you feel unwell, and ask your doctor why you need it and which signs afterwards need a hospital.

# What a transfusion is

Blood from a donor is separated into parts, so one donation can help more than one patient. A person is given the part they need:

- red cells, for anaemia, when there are too few red cells to carry oxygen
- platelets, to help the blood clot
- plasma, the fluid part of blood, also to help the blood clot

Sometimes whole blood is given.

# Why it may be needed

- losing a lot of blood in an accident, an operation, or while giving birth
- a condition that affects the red cells or the bone marrow, such as thalassemia, sickle cell disease or aplastic anaemia
- treatment such as chemotherapy or radiotherapy
- a condition in which the blood does not clot well, so a person bleeds a lot or for longer

A transfusion is not a better choice than treating the cause. It is used to manage an emergency, such as heavy blood loss or anaemia that is putting the body in danger, and in a few conditions where the body cannot make enough blood, such as thalassemia major. It does not treat the reason the haemoglobin is low. That cause, such as too little iron or vitamin B12, blood loss, or an illness of the bone marrow, needs to be found and treated, or the anaemia comes back.

Ask the doctor about other choices. A transfusion given when it is not needed brings risk without benefit, and uses blood another patient may need.

# What happens

## Before

- A doctor or nurse explains why the transfusion is needed and answers questions.
- A blood sample is taken to check that the donor blood is the right group and a good match for you.
- You are usually asked to sign a consent form. Ask the team to explain anything you do not understand before you sign. In an emergency, such as during an operation, it may not be possible to ask you first.

## During

- You sit in a chair or lie on a bed.
- A thin plastic tube is put into a vein in your arm and joined to the bag of blood, called a drip.
- The blood flows in slowly.
- Your pulse, temperature and blood pressure are checked before, during and after.
- It usually takes one to four hours, sometimes longer, depending on how much is needed.

## After

You can usually go home unless you are very unwell. Many people start to feel better within a day. Before you leave, the team tells you which side effects to look out for.

# Is the blood safe?

Donated blood is tested for infections before it is used. The World Health Organization says every donation should be screened for HIV, hepatitis B, hepatitis C and syphilis. In India, blood is also tested for malaria. Blood is also carefully handled and stored, so the chance of catching an infection from a transfusion is low. It is not zero.

The World Health Organization also says that regular voluntary donors who are not paid are the safest donors, because infections in the blood are least common among them.

# Side effects and reactions

Many people have no side effects. Some have mild ones:

- a fever, or feeling hot, cold or shivery
- a rash

Tell the nurse at once. Slowing down or stopping the transfusion usually helps, and you may be given paracetamol for a fever or an antihistamine for a rash.

A serious allergic reaction is rare. Tell the team immediately if you have any trouble breathing.

Other problems are rare but can happen hours, days or even weeks later:

- an infection, which is unlikely because the blood is tested, but the chance is not zero
- the body reacting against the donor's blood cells
- heart or lung problems
- too much iron in the body, if a person has many transfusions

People who need regular transfusions, such as children with thalassemia major, have more to plan for, including removing extra iron (chelation). The Thalassemia chapter on this site has a part on regular transfusions.

The young man tells the nurse about the shivering. She stops the platelets, checks his temperature and blood pressure, and calls the doctor. He is given paracetamol, the reaction settles, and the team notes it in his file for the next time.

# Go to a hospital now if

Go at once, and do not drive yourself, if you have had a transfusion recently and you have:

- severe difficulty breathing
- tingling or swelling of the mouth or throat
- chest pain
- a fever, or feeling hot, cold or shivery
- aching muscles for no reason, or lower back pain
- nausea or vomiting
- blood in the urine, very little urine, or very dark urine
- bleeding from the mouth, nose, back passage or eyelids
- yellow eyes or skin

Tell them you recently had a blood transfusion.

# Take these questions to your doctor

- Why do I need a transfusion, and what is causing the low haemoglobin?
- Which part of the blood will I get: red cells, platelets or plasma?
- How long will it take, and can I go home after?
- What should I tell the nurse during the transfusion?
- Which signs after it mean I should come back?
- Will I need more transfusions?

# Common questions

## Can I get HIV or hepatitis from a transfusion?

The chance is low, but it is not zero. Donated blood is tested for infections before it is used. The World Health Organization says every donation should be screened for HIV, hepatitis B, hepatitis C and syphilis, and in India it is also tested for malaria. You can ask the hospital whether the blood was tested.

## I started shivering during a transfusion. What does it mean?

A fever, shivering or a rash can happen. Tell the nurse at once. Slowing down or stopping the transfusion usually helps, and the team checks you.

## Does the blood have to match my group?

Yes. A blood sample is taken before the transfusion to check that the donor blood is the right group and a good match.
