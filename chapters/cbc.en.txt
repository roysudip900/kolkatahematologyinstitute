# What a blood count measures

The complete blood count, or CBC, is one of the most common blood tests, and it is often done as part of a routine check-up. It counts the three kinds of cells in the blood: red cells, which carry oxygen; white cells, which fight infection; and platelets, which help stop bleeding. Each line on the report shows your result, and beside it the range the lab uses.

# Reading the lines

- Hemoglobin is the part of the red cells that carries oxygen.
- Hematocrit, often printed as PCV, is how much of the blood is made of red cells.
- MCV is the average size of the red cells.
- White cells, printed as WBC or TLC, are the cells that fight infection.
- Platelets are tiny pieces in the blood that help stop bleeding.

What a high or low value means for you depends on the other lines, your age, how you feel and your past reports. The same letter can mean very different things in two people, so leave the meaning to your doctor. Do not start iron, a tonic or any other medicine because of a report, and do not stop one either. Show the report to your doctor first.

# Why the ranges differ

There is no single normal for everyone. Ranges differ between men and women, between children and adults, and with things like altitude. They also differ a little from one lab to another, even with the same machines. That is why each report prints its own range, and why you should read your result against the range on that report, not one from the internet.

# One letter beside a number

A lab sets its range so that most healthy people fall inside it. About one healthy person in twenty falls outside each range. So on a report with twenty lines, a healthy person can expect one H or L.

A value just outside the range is often nothing. What matters more is how far outside it is, whether more than one kind of cell is out of range together, whether it keeps moving on a second test, and how you feel.

# When to see a hematologist

Your own doctor can read most reports. A hematologist is usually needed when two or three kinds of cells are abnormal together, when a value is far outside the range, when the numbers keep changing on repeat tests, or when the report goes with bleeding, repeated fevers, or weight loss. Sometimes the doctor will ask for a smear, where the blood is looked at under a microscope.

Bring your old reports too. The same line on last year's report can tell the doctor a lot.

# How the story goes on

The doctor reads the whole sheet, with the boy's fever and how he is now. Some of it needs nothing more. For the rest, the doctor asks for a repeat test in a few weeks and explains what will be looked for. The mother goes home with a plan instead of a list of frightening words.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- the report shows very low hemoglobin and the person is breathless, has chest pain or faints
- bleeding that will not stop, or new purple spots, with a low platelet count
- fever in someone who has been told their white cells are very low
- the doctor or lab calls you about a result and asks you to come at once

# Take these questions to your doctor

Bring this report and any old ones, and ask:

- Which of these results matter, and which can I ignore?
- Is this value far outside the range, or just outside?
- Should any test be repeated, and when?
- Do I need to see a hematologist?
- Does anyone else in my family need a test?

# Common questions

## My report has an H or an L. Is it serious?

Often not. About one healthy person in twenty falls outside any one range. Your doctor looks at how far outside it is and at the rest of the report.

## Why do two labs give different normal ranges?

Each lab sets its own range for its methods, and ranges also differ with sex and age. Read your result against the range printed on your own report.

## When should I see a hematologist about my blood report?

When more than one kind of cell is abnormal, when a value is far outside the range or keeps changing, or when the report goes with bleeding, fevers or weight loss. Your doctor can refer you.
