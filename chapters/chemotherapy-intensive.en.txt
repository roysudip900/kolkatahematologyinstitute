# Why it is called intensive

Intensive chemotherapy uses a combination of strong medicines to kill the leukaemia cells in the blood and the bone marrow, with the aim of curing the disease. Whether it is offered depends on the type of leukaemia, the person's age and their general health.

The same medicines that clear the leukaemia also stop the marrow making healthy blood for a while. Red cells, white cells and platelets all fall very low, and for some days the body cannot fight germs or stop bleeding well on its own. This is why the first phase is usually done in hospital, where the team can watch closely and act fast.

# The phases

Treatment comes in phases, each with its own job.

- The first phase aims to clear the leukaemia from the blood and marrow. When the blood count has recovered and the marrow test shows almost no leukaemia cells, the leukaemia is in remission.
- The next phase kills leukaemia cells that may be hiding and could grow back.
- In acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, a long third phase follows, mostly tablets at home with regular check-ups. Taking these as the doctor orders lowers the chance of the leukaemia coming back.

Remission is good news, but it is not the end of treatment. The later phases are what stop the leukaemia returning, so the plan goes on even when the child is running around and the reports look fine.

In acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, chemotherapy is also put into the fluid around the spinal cord, through a needle in the lower back, to stop leukaemia cells spreading to the brain. Some people later need a stem cell or bone marrow transplant, or targeted medicines or immunotherapy, if the leukaemia is likely to come back or comes back.

# The weeks on the ward

Much of the work in hospital is keeping the body safe until the marrow recovers. This is called supportive care.

- Red cell and platelet transfusions replace the blood cells the leukaemia and the treatment have destroyed, until the marrow makes its own again.
- Antibiotics and antifungal medicines prevent or treat infections.
- Some people get medicines called growth factors that help the marrow make blood cells.

Needing blood and platelets often during these weeks is expected. It does not mean the treatment is failing.

# What it asks of a family

Someone usually stays close, and the earning and the travelling have to be shared out. Before the first phase, ask the team what the family can do: washing hands before touching the patient, keeping visitors with a cold or cough away, and asking before bringing food from home. Ask too about a place to stay near the hospital, leave from work, and who to call at night.

Tell the specialists about any symptom or side effect that worries you. You do not need to wait for the next check-up.

# How the stories go on

The boy spends his first weeks on the ward, with transfusions on some days and a fever once, treated quickly. His leukaemia goes into remission. The tablets at home come later, and his mother learns which signs mean the bus back to Kolkata that same day.

The man's brother runs the shop. After the first phase his leukaemia is in remission, and his team talks to him about the next phase and whether a stem cell transplant is right for him.

Whichever story is closer to yours, ask the team to draw the plan out on paper, phase by phase, before the first one starts.

# Go to a hospital now if

During intensive treatment, and in the weeks at home between phases, go at once if:

- there is a fever, or you feel hot, cold or shivery
- breathing is difficult or very fast
- there is confusion, slurred speech, or a child is unusually sleepy and hard to wake
- the skin, lips or tongue look blue, grey, pale or blotchy
- very little urine is passed
- there is bleeding that will not stop, or new bruises or tiny red spots
- the mouth or throat is so sore that you cannot swallow or drink

Tell the hospital that you are on intensive chemotherapy for leukaemia.

# Take these questions to your doctor

Your hematologist will want you to understand the plan. Ask:

- What is the aim of my treatment, and how many phases will there be?
- How long is the first stay in hospital likely to be?
- Will I need transfusions, and what are they for?
- Which signs mean I must come back at once, and where do I go at night?
- Is a stem cell or bone marrow transplant likely to be part of my plan?

# Common questions

## What does remission mean in leukaemia?

It means the blood count has recovered and the marrow test shows almost no leukaemia cells. Treatment still continues, because the later phases stop the leukaemia coming back.

## Why does my child need so many blood transfusions during chemo?

The leukaemia and the chemotherapy stop the marrow making enough blood for a while. Transfusions replace the red cells and platelets until it recovers. It does not mean the treatment is failing.

## Is intensive chemotherapy given to everyone with leukaemia?

No. It depends on the type of leukaemia, the person's age and their general health. When it is not right for someone, there is lower-intensity treatment that aims to control the leukaemia.
