The son wants to know why his mother is not getting the strongest treatment. The wife wants to know whether palliative means the end.

# What lower-intensity treatment aims to do

Some blood cancers can be treated in two ways. Intensive treatment aims to cure, but it is hard on the body. Lower-intensity treatment aims to keep the cancer under control for as long as possible, ease the symptoms it causes, and help the person live longer. It is usually given at home or as a day visit, and it has fewer side effects.

Which one is right depends on the type of cancer, the person's age and their general health. For someone older, or with other serious illnesses, strong treatment can do more harm than good. Choosing the gentler path is a medical decision made for that person.

The treatment can be chemotherapy, a targeted medicine, or both, and the plan is different for each person. When a cancer cannot be cured, the aim becomes to limit it and its symptoms and help the person live longer.

# Palliative care is not giving up

Palliative care means care that improves quality of life for someone with a serious illness. It treats the symptoms and the side effects, as early as possible, and it looks after worry, sleep, money troubles and the family too. A palliative care team of doctors, nurses and others works alongside the hematologist.

It can begin at any stage of the illness, together with treatment aimed at the cancer, and anyone can have it, whatever their age. Care at the very end of life is only one part of it. Starting palliative care early can mean fewer unnecessary hospital stays, and for people with advanced cancer it can improve quality of life and mood.

# Pain and breathlessness can be treated

Pain and breathlessness are two of the commonest troubles in serious illness, and both can be eased. For strong pain, morphine and medicines like it are essential, and they also ease breathlessness. Tell the team if a medicine is not working or is causing trouble, and never change it yourself.

# Deciding together

Good care starts with talking about what matters most to the person: staying at home, being free of pain, a family event, fewer trips to hospital. The team can help the family talk about this openly, and about wishes for the future, so that decisions are made calmly and not in the middle of the night.

Families carry a great deal. Many carers feel worn out from looking after someone while still running the house and going to work. Tell the team when it gets too much; helping the carer is part of palliative care.

# How the stories go on

The grandmother has her treatment as day visits, with transfusions on some days when her blood is low. She sleeps at home, eats her own fish curry and sits on the verandah in the afternoon. Her son comes with her to every visit and asks the doctor his questions himself.

The man meets the palliative care team alongside his hematologist. His pain medicine is adjusted, he sleeps better, and his breathlessness on the stairs is eased. On the day of the annaprashan he is there, resting in a chair between the rituals.

Whichever story is closer to yours, ask the team what the treatment is aiming for, and what you can expect from it.

# Go to a hospital now if

Go at once, and tell the staff about the treatment, if:

- there is a fever, or you feel hot, cold or shivery
- there is bleeding that will not stop, or blood in vomit, urine or stool
- breathing becomes suddenly worse, or is hard even at rest
- pain suddenly becomes much worse and the usual medicines do not help
- there is confusion or the person is very hard to wake
- the person cannot drink, or passes very little urine

# Take these questions to your doctor

Your hematologist will want to hear what matters most to you. Ask:

- What is the aim of this treatment: to cure, to control, or to ease symptoms?
- Why is lower-intensity treatment advised for me rather than intensive treatment?
- Which side effects should I expect, and where will I have it?
- Can we meet a palliative care team now, alongside the treatment?
- What should we do if pain or breathlessness gets worse at night?

# Common questions

## Does palliative care mean the patient is dying?

No. Palliative care can begin at any stage, alongside treatment for the cancer. It aims to help the person feel better and live as well as possible.

## Is lower-intensity chemotherapy worth having?

It aims to keep the cancer under control for as long as possible and ease its symptoms, with fewer side effects than intensive treatment. Ask the hematologist what it can do in your case.

## Will morphine make my father addicted?

Morphine is one of the essential medicines for strong pain in serious illness. Talk to the doctor openly about the worry, and do not change or stop it on your own.
