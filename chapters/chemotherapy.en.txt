> Chemotherapy uses medicines that kill fast-growing cells, and for many blood cancers it is a main treatment that can cure, control the disease or ease symptoms. Its side effects usually settle after treatment ends, but a fever during chemotherapy is an emergency: go to the hospital at once, and never stop or skip a cycle on your own.

The word "chemo" made the whole family go quiet.

He is fifty-six, a retired school clerk in Barasat. The lump in his neck turned out to be lymphoma, and the hematologist said chemotherapy. That evening his brother-in-law phoned: chemo burns the body from inside, people come home thinner than when they went in, why not try the kabiraj in their village first? His wife hid the hospital papers in the almirah and did not sleep.

In a Kolkata ward, a woman of twenty-five has acute leukaemia. She started her first cycle the day after the diagnosis. She is less scared of the drip than of the mirror. Her wedding was fixed for the winter, and she keeps asking the same two things: will her hair come back, and will she still be able to have a baby?

He wants to know whether chemo will do more harm than the cancer. She wants to know what it will leave behind.

# What chemotherapy does

Chemotherapy is treatment with medicines that kill cancer cells or stop them growing. It works on cells that divide fast. Cancer cells divide fast, but so do some healthy cells: the roots of the hair, the lining of the mouth and gut, and the bone marrow, where blood is made. That is why the side effects show up in those places, and why most of them get better once treatment stops and the healthy cells grow back.

It can cure some cancers, stop them coming back, slow them down or ease the symptoms they cause. In blood cancers it is often given with targeted medicines, immunotherapy or a bone marrow transplant, and the plan is made for each person.

# How it is given

Most chemotherapy goes into a vein through a drip, from a few minutes to a few days at a time. Some is a tablet taken at home, and some is an injection under the skin. People who need many drips may have a thin tube placed in a large vein in the chest, so the arm is not pricked every time.

It is usually given in cycles: a few days of treatment, then a break of days or weeks so the body can recover and make new healthy cells. Before each cycle there is a blood test. Some treatment, such as the first treatment for acute leukaemia, usually needs a stay in hospital. Much of the rest is given as a day visit.

# The hair, the sickness and the tiredness

Most people get some side effects, but nobody gets all of them. The common ones are tiredness, feeling sick, a sore mouth, constipation or loose motions, and hair loss. With some medicines, the hands and feet tingle.

Hair loss is usually temporary. Hair often grows back within two or three months after treatment ends, soft and fine at first, sometimes curlier or a different colour.

Tell the team about every side effect: there are medicines to stop the sickness and ease a sore mouth. Help with meals and housework on the day of treatment and the day after makes the tiredness easier.

# The days when the white cells are low

Chemotherapy lowers the white cells that fight germs, and in every cycle there are days when they are at their lowest. An infection then can quickly become life-threatening. This is why a fever during chemotherapy is an emergency, even if it feels like an ordinary cold.

Do not take a fever medicine at home and wait to see. It can bring the temperature down and hide a serious infection, so ask the team before taking any medicine for fever. Washing hands well, eating only well cooked fish, meat and eggs, and keeping away from people with a cold or a cough all lower the risk.

# Children, marriage and pregnancy

Some chemotherapy medicines can lower fertility, for a while or for good. If having a baby one day matters, ask about it before treatment starts, because there may be a way to store eggs or sperm first. The team may not raise it on their own, so it is fine to ask.

Chemotherapy can harm an unborn baby. A woman who might be pregnant must tell the team before treatment, and couples need to use contraception during treatment.

# Kabiraji, tonics and home remedies

Some herbal medicines and supplements can harm people having chemotherapy or stop the cancer medicines working as well as they should. Tell the hematologist about every tonic, herbal medicine, homeopathic medicine or supplement before taking it. Do not stop or delay chemotherapy to try something else first.

# How the stories go on

The man's family meets the hematologist together and asks every question on their list: which side effects to expect, which days to be careful, what to do if a fever comes. He has his treatment as a day patient, rests the day after, and keeps every appointment.

The woman spends the first weeks in hospital. Her hair falls, and her sister cuts it short. She asks about storing eggs, and the team tells her what is possible in her case. When her treatment ends, the hair starts coming back, soft at first.

Whichever story is closer to yours, write your questions down and take them to the hematologist before the first cycle.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

During chemotherapy, go at once if:

- there is a fever, or you feel hot, cold, sweaty or shivery, even without a thermometer
- there are other signs of infection: sore throat, cough, burning when passing urine, loose motions, or redness or swelling where the tube goes in
- you become breathless suddenly, have chest pain, or cough up blood
- an arm or leg becomes painful, swollen and warm
- there is bleeding that will not stop, or many bruises without injury

Tell the hospital staff that you are having chemotherapy.

# Take these questions to your doctor

Your hematologist expects questions. These are a good start:

- What is the aim of my chemotherapy: to cure, to control, or to ease symptoms?
- How will it be given, and will I need to stay in hospital?
- Which side effects should I expect, and which ones mean I must come at once?
- On which days will my white cells be lowest?
- Will it affect my chance of having children, and can anything be done before we start?

# Common questions

## Does hair grow back after chemotherapy?

Usually yes, often within two or three months after treatment ends. It may be finer, curlier or a different colour at first.

## Can I work during chemotherapy?

Many people can, if they plan work around how they feel. Keep the day of treatment and the day after for rest, and ask your employer about lighter days.

## If the side effects are mild, does it mean chemo is not working?

No. Side effects do not show how well chemotherapy is working. The doctor checks that with an examination, blood tests and scans.
