# What a bruise is

A bruise is blood that has leaked under the skin from a small blood vessel, usually after a knock. Normally, tiny cells in the blood called platelets, and proteins called clotting factors, seal the leak quickly, so the bruise stays small and fades. When there are too few platelets, or a clotting factor is missing, the leak goes on longer, and bruises come easily, grow large, or appear without any knock.

Three kinds of marks are worth knowing:

- a bruise: a blue, purple or black patch that turns green and yellow as it fades
- petechiae: tiny flat red or purple dots, like pinpricks
- purpura: bigger red, purple or brownish patches of bleeding under the skin

# When bruises are from play

Children who run, climb and play football get bruises, mostly on the parts that bump into things, such as the shins and knees. A bruise that has a clear cause, on a child who is otherwise well, usually needs nothing but time.

# When bruises need a doctor

Show your child to a doctor if:

- bruises come without a knock, or keep coming in new places
- bruises are large, or appear on the back, tummy, chest or face
- there are tiny red or purple dots, or purple patches
- there are nosebleeds that come often or last long, or bleeding gums
- a joint becomes swollen and painful without a clear injury
- the child is also pale, tired, feverish, has bone pain or swollen glands

These can be signs of low platelets, as in immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), which in children can follow a viral infection; of a bleeding disorder such as haemophilia or von Willebrand disease, which can run in families; of dengue after a fever; or, rarely, of leukaemia. A rash of raised red or purple spots on the legs and bottom can also come from Henoch-Schönlein purpura, an inflammation of the blood vessels that is commoner in children.

A blood count, and sometimes clotting tests, show the doctor which it is. Tell the doctor about bleeding in the family, and about any medicine the child has taken.

# What not to do at home

- Do not give painkillers such as ibuprofen or aspirin for the pain or a fever without asking the doctor, because they can make bleeding worse.
- Do not rub ointments into the patches or wait for them to fade before the child has been seen.
- Do not give steroid medicine or any tonic on your own.

Whichever story is closer to yours, look at where the bruises are, whether there was a knock, and what else is going on, and tell the doctor.

# Go to a hospital now if

Go at once, and do not wait for the spots to fade, if:

- the child has tiny red or purple spots or patches and a fever, or seems unwell, drowsy or hard to wake
- the spots or patches do not fade when a glass is pressed against them and the child is unwell
- a nosebleed lasts more than ten to fifteen minutes, or bleeding from a cut or the gums will not stop
- there is blood in the urine, blood in vomit, or black stool
- there is a head injury, a bad headache or vomiting in a child with many bruises or spots
- the child is very pale, breathless or has a swollen, painful joint

# Take these questions to your doctor

A doctor or hematologist can sort this out quickly, so bring these questions:

- Are these bruises from play, or do they need tests?
- What do the blood count and clotting tests show?
- Are the platelets low, and why?
- Does anyone in the family need to be tested?
- Which activities should my child avoid for now?
- Which medicines must my child not take?

# Common questions

## My child gets bruises easily. Is that normal?

Bruises on the shins and knees of an active child are common. Bruises without a knock, on the back or tummy, or with tiny red dots or nosebleeds, need a doctor and a blood test.

## What are the tiny red spots on my child's legs?

They may be petechiae, small spots of bleeding under the skin, which can come from low platelets or other causes. If they come with fever or the child is unwell, go to the hospital at once.

## Can I give my child painkillers for a bruise?

Ask the doctor first. Ibuprofen and aspirin can make bleeding worse in a child whose platelets or clotting are low.
