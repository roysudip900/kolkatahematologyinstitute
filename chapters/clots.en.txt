# What a blood clot is

A clot is the body's seal to stop bleeding from a wound. When it forms inside a vein where there is no wound, it can block the blood flow.

- A DVT (deep vein thrombosis) is a clot in a deep vein, usually in the leg, sometimes in the arm.
- A pulmonary embolism (PE) happens when a clot travels to the lungs and blocks a blood vessel there. It can be life-threatening if not treated quickly.

# Who is more likely to get one

A clot is more likely:

- during or after a stay in hospital, especially when you cannot move around much, and after an operation
- when you are in bed for several days, or on a long journey by bus, train, car or plane
- during pregnancy and in the weeks after having a baby
- with age over sixty, extra weight, smoking, or being short of water
- with cancer, heart failure, or a past clot
- with the combined contraceptive pill or hormone treatment that contains oestrogen
- when the blood clots more easily than usual

Sometimes a clot happens for no clear reason.

# Signs of a clot

In the leg (DVT):

- throbbing pain in one leg, usually the calf or thigh
- swelling in one leg
- red, blue or darker skin over the painful area
- swollen veins

In the lungs (PE):

- breathlessness that comes on suddenly
- chest pain that is worse when you breathe in
- coughing up blood

# What treatment can do

Most clots are treated with blood-thinning medicines, called anticoagulants. They do not actually thin the blood. They help stop the clot getting bigger and help stop new clots forming.

## The blood thinners

- Tablets: the newer ones, rivaroxaban, apixaban, dabigatran and edoxaban, and the older one, warfarin.
- Injections: heparin, often used first in hospital and in pregnancy, because warfarin and the newer tablets are not used in pregnancy.

The newer tablets are usually preferred over warfarin. They may not suit people with serious kidney or liver disease or some other conditions, so the doctor chooses.

Many people with a DVT without complications, or a small PE at low risk, can be treated at home rather than in hospital, if they have support at home and can get their medicines. Others need a hospital stay.

The first part of treatment usually lasts three to six months. Then the doctor decides whether to stop or continue. When the clot followed a short-term cause, such as an operation or an injury, the blood thinner is usually stopped. When there was no clear cause, or the cause continues, many people keep taking it for the long term, with regular check-ups to weigh the benefit against the risk of bleeding. The doctor decides which medicine, how much and for how long.

## Never stop a blood thinner on your own

Stopping too early can let the clot grow or a new one form. Only stop on the advice of your doctor. Before any operation, endoscopy or tooth extraction, tell the doctor or dentist that you take a blood thinner. They will tell you what to do, which may or may not mean stopping it for a short time.

## Living with a blood thinner

- Ask your doctor or pharmacist before taking any other medicine, remedy or supplement, including medicines bought from a shop and herbal remedies. Some antibiotics, painkillers and other medicines change how blood thinners work.
- For pain, ask first. Ibuprofen and aspirin add to the risk of bleeding. If you already take aspirin for your heart, the doctor may decide to pause it while you take a blood thinner. Do not stop it yourself.
- On warfarin, you will have regular blood tests to check how fast your blood clots, and the doctor adjusts the medicine. Keep the amount of green leafy vegetables you eat steady from week to week, and avoid large amounts of alcohol.
- Use a soft toothbrush and take care when shaving, gardening or playing sport, to avoid cuts.
- If you are pregnant or planning a baby, talk to the doctor first, because most blood thinners are not suitable in pregnancy.

## Other treatments

Most DVTs need a blood thinner alone. For a PE that strains the heart and drops the blood pressure, doctors give a medicine that dissolves the clot, in hospital, along with the blood thinner. In a few cases they break up the clot through a thin tube. A small filter placed in a large vein, which catches clots before they reach the lungs, is used only for people who cannot take blood thinners, and is usually removed when they can.

Compression stockings are not needed by everyone after a DVT, but they may ease swelling and pain in some people. Some people have pain and swelling in the leg that lasts long after a DVT, and a few develop long-term high pressure in the lung vessels after a PE, which causes breathlessness. Tell the doctor if this happens.

## Getting better and preventing another clot

- Stay active and walk regularly if you can.
- Keep the affected leg raised when you sit.
- Put off flights and long journeys until at least two weeks after starting the blood thinner, and ask your doctor when it is safe.
- On a long journey, get up and walk when you can, move your legs and feet often while sitting, drink water, and wear loose clothes.
- Stay a healthy weight, do not smoke, and avoid too much alcohol.
- Before any operation or hospital stay, ask how your risk of a clot will be lowered.

# Go to a hospital now if

Go at once, and do not drive yourself, if you have:

- breathlessness that comes on suddenly
- chest pain or upper back pain
- a very fast heartbeat, or fainting
- pain and swelling in the leg together with breathlessness or chest pain

If you take a blood thinner, also go at once for:

- blood in vomit, coughing up blood, or black or bloody stools
- a nosebleed or any bleeding that will not stop
- a hard knock to the head, or a serious accident
- a sudden severe headache or sudden severe back pain

# Take these questions to your doctor

- Why did I get this clot, and do I need more tests?
- Which blood thinner, and for how long?
- What should I do if I forget to take it?
- Which painkillers and other medicines are safe for me?
- What should I tell my dentist or surgeon?
- How can I lower my risk on a long journey or during my next pregnancy?

# Common questions

## Is a swollen leg after having a baby a worry?

The weeks after having a baby carry a higher chance of a clot. Pain and swelling in one leg, especially the calf, needs a doctor soon.

## Can I stop my blood thinner when I feel well?

No. Only stop on your doctor's advice. Feeling well does not mean the risk has gone.

## Do blood thinners make the blood thin?

No. They slow down how the blood clots, which is why bleeding is the main side effect.
