> Heavy periods (menorrhagia) mean bleeding that soaks a pad every hour or two or lasts more than seven days, and in a young girl they can sometimes be the first sign of a bleeding disorder such as von Willebrand disease. Show your doctor or a hematologist, ask for a blood test for anaemia, and go to the hospital at once if she faints or cannot stop bleeding.

In Kolkata, a girl of fifteen has periods that last ten days. She also gets nosebleeds that take a long time to stop. Her mother has had heavy periods all her life, and an aunt needed blood after giving birth. The family thinks it simply runs in the women of the house.

The grandmother wants to know whether to wait. The mother wants to know whether heavy bleeding can run in a family.

# When periods are heavy

Periods may be irregular in the first months, and they can be heavier around the time they start. But bleeding is too heavy if a girl:

- needs a new pad every hour or two, or two pads or products at once
- bleeds for more than seven days
- passes large clots
- bleeds through to her clothes or bedding
- misses school, games or tuition because of her period
- feels tired or breathless a lot

None of this is something a girl should just put up with.

# Why it matters to a blood doctor

In the first few years after periods start, the commonest reason for heavy bleeding is that the ovaries do not yet release an egg every month, so the hormones are not settled. That is why families, and sometimes doctors, call it hormonal and wait. But in a study of two hundred girls sent to specialists in the United States for heavy periods, one in three had a bleeding disorder, and it was found just as often when the bleeding looked hormonal. Many of them had waited years for the diagnosis.

Some girls bleed heavily because their blood does not clot properly. One of these is von Willebrand disease, in which a protein that helps blood clot is low or does not work well. It is inherited, so it runs in families. The signs are easy to miss: bruising easily, nosebleeds that come often or last long, bleeding gums, bleeding more than usual after a cut or a dental or other procedure, and heavy periods. Sometimes heavy periods are the first sign anyone notices.

Other causes include low platelets, some medicines such as blood thinners and chemotherapy, and conditions of the womb or ovaries. The doctor asks about all of these.

# The anaemia that comes with it

Heavy periods are a very common cause of iron deficiency anaemia. Each heavy period takes away iron. She may be pale, tired, breathless on the stairs, unable to concentrate in class, or faint in the heat.

Iron can treat the anaemia, but it does not stop the bleeding. The cause of the heavy periods still has to be found and treated. A blood transfusion is only for an emergency, when the bleeding or the anaemia is severe, and it does not treat the cause either.

# How it is found

The doctor asks how long the periods last, how often she changes pads, whether she has clots or floods, and about bruises, nosebleeds and bleeding in the family. A period diary kept for a few months helps the doctor see what happens. Guidelines for girls with heavy periods ask the doctor to check three things: anaemia and iron stores, hormone causes, and a bleeding disorder, working with a hematologist. Blood tests check for anaemia and iron, and clotting tests look for a bleeding disorder. Testing for von Willebrand disease may need to be done more than once, and a hematologist usually arranges it.

# What treatment can do

Heavy periods can be treated, and the plan depends on the cause.

- Medicines can make the bleeding less, such as tranexamic acid, or hormone treatment such as the pill.
- When a bleeding disorder is found, the hematologist may add other treatment for bleeding, before a tooth is pulled or an operation, or when bleeding is heavy.
- Iron tablets help refill the iron the bleeding took away.

Which medicine, and for how long, is decided by the doctor for each girl. Do not start or stop any medicine, including the pill, on your own.

# How the stories go on

The thirteen-year-old's mother takes her to a doctor after the fainting. Her blood test shows anaemia from iron loss. The doctor treats the anaemia, starts medicine to reduce the bleeding, and refers her to a hematologist to look for a bleeding disorder. She keeps a diary of each period, and goes back to tuition.

The fifteen-year-old sees a hematologist with her mother. Tests over a few weeks show von Willebrand disease. She learns which medicine to use for a heavy period or a nosebleed, which medicines are safe for her, and to tell any dentist or surgeon about it. Her mother is tested too.

Whichever story is closer to yours, write down how long the periods last and how often the pad is changed, and take it to the doctor.

# Go to a hospital now if

Go at once, and do not wait for the bleeding to settle, if:

- she faints, or feels dizzy and cannot stand
- she is very pale, breathless or has a racing heartbeat
- the bleeding soaks a pad every hour, hour after hour
- a nosebleed lasts more than ten to fifteen minutes, or bleeding from a cut will not stop
- there is blood in the urine, or black or dark red stool

# Take these questions to your doctor

A doctor or hematologist can help, so bring these questions:

- Are her periods heavier than they should be?
- Does she have anaemia, and does she need iron?
- Could she have a bleeding disorder such as von Willebrand disease?
- Should anyone else in the family be tested?
- Which treatment will reduce the bleeding, and what should we do in an emergency?
- Does she need to tell a dentist or surgeon before any procedure?

# Common questions

## Is heavy bleeding normal in the first periods?

Periods can be irregular and sometimes heavier when they first start, usually because of hormones. But a bleeding disorder can hide behind the same pattern, and bleeding that soaks a pad every hour or two, lasts more than seven days or makes her faint is not something to wait out. See a doctor.

## Will heavy periods settle after marriage?

Waiting for marriage does not treat the cause, and the anaemia can grow worse meanwhile. If there is a bleeding disorder, she needs to know before childbirth or any operation. See a doctor now.

## Can heavy periods run in the family?

Yes, sometimes. Von Willebrand disease usually runs in families, so if a mother, sister or aunt has heavy periods or bleeds a lot, tell the doctor.
