Every evening the father takes a photo of the new report and sends it to the family group.

His daughter is nine. It is the fourth day of fever, and the platelet count is lower than yesterday. The phone does not stop. One aunt says papaya leaf juice, another says goat's milk, a neighbour brings kiwis. His brother-in-law asks why the doctor has not given her platelets yet. By night the father has decided that if the count drops again, he will insist on a transfusion.

On the fifth morning the fever is gone. Everyone relaxes, and the aunt says the papaya leaf worked.

The family is doing what loving families do. The number they watch is only one of the things that matter, and the day they relaxed is the day that needs the closest watching.

# How dengue usually goes

Dengue is spread by mosquito bites. A few days after the bite comes a high fever, with headache, pain behind the eyes, body aches, sometimes vomiting and a rash. The fever usually lasts a few days.

Most people get better in one to two weeks. A smaller number develop severe dengue, and that is what everyone is trying to catch in time.

The severe signs often come after the fever has gone away. So keep watching closely on the day the fever breaks and in the days after. A second dengue infection, in a later year, carries a higher risk of severe dengue.

The platelet count is often at its lowest around the fourth to sixth day of the illness, about when the risky days tend to come, and then climbs back over the following days.

After recovery, tiredness can last several weeks, then it passes.

# The platelet number

In dengue the platelet count often falls, because the body makes fewer platelets and destroys more of them. A slow fall is common. A fast fall, especially with a rising haematocrit (PCV) on the same report, is one of the warning signs doctors look for. That is why each blood test the doctor asks for matters.

The doctor watches more than the count: the warning signs, how much the child drinks, and how they look. A child who is drinking well, passing urine and playing a little tells the doctor a great deal, often more than a single number on a report.

Many families think a low count means platelets must be given. Giving platelets just because the count is low has not been shown to prevent bleeding, and transfusions can cause harm of their own. Doctors give platelets when there is serious bleeding or a high risk of it. In a study of children treated for dengue in Odisha, bleeding did not go with the platelet count, and platelets were given only to a few children with severe dengue. If you are worried about the count, ask the doctor what they are watching for, and why.

# Caring for someone with dengue at home

- Rest, and plenty to drink: water, oral rehydration solution, soup, coconut water, whatever the child will take.
- For fever and pain, use the medicine your doctor advises, usually paracetamol, in the amount the doctor tells you.
- Do not give ibuprofen or aspirin. They can make bleeding more likely. If someone in the family takes aspirin or a blood thinner from their doctor, tell that doctor about the dengue rather than stopping it on your own.
- There is no specific medicine for dengue itself. Papaya leaf juice, goat's milk and kiwi are often suggested to raise platelets. Ask the doctor before giving any of them, and never let them take the place of drinks and check-ups.
- Keep every blood test the doctor asks for, and the next visit.

The mosquitoes that spread dengue bite during the day, so use a net for daytime sleep, and empty and clean water containers every week.

# How the story goes on

The doctor explains that the count often falls in dengue, that they are watching how fast it falls along with the rest of the report, and that the days after the fever goes matter more than any single number. The family stops sending reports to the group and starts watching the girl instead: how much she drinks, whether she passes urine, whether her stomach hurts.

She has no warning signs. Her count turns around on its own and climbs over the following days, and she needs no transfusion. She is tired for a few weeks, then back at school. The aunt still credits the papaya leaf.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- severe stomach pain, or vomiting again and again
- bleeding from the gums or nose, blood in vomit, stool or urine, or new bruises
- fast breathing
- the person is very tired, weak or restless
- cold, pale skin, or great thirst

These can come during the fever or after it goes. If any appear, go at once.

# Take these questions to your doctor

- Which day of the illness is this, and when is the risky time for us?
- What should we watch for at home, and when should we come back?
- How much should my child drink in a day?
- Which medicine can we give for fever, and which should we avoid?
- Does my child need a platelet transfusion, and why or why not?

# Common questions

## When is dengue most dangerous?

Often after the fever goes away. That is when the warning signs need the closest watching.

## Does papaya leaf juice raise platelets?

The World Health Organization says there is no specific treatment for dengue. Ask the doctor before giving papaya leaf juice, and never let it replace drinks or check-ups.

## Does every dengue patient with low platelets need a platelet transfusion?

No. A falling count is common in dengue. Doctors give platelets when there is serious bleeding or a high risk of it, not for the number alone.
