# How dengue usually goes

Dengue is spread by mosquito bites. A few days after the bite comes a sudden high fever, with headache, pain behind the eyes, body aches, sometimes vomiting and a rash. The fever usually lasts a few days.

Most people get better in one to two weeks. A smaller number develop severe dengue, and that is what everyone is trying to catch in time.

The severe signs often come after the fever has gone away. So keep watching closely on the day the fever breaks and in the days just after. A second dengue infection, in a later year, carries a higher risk of severe dengue.

After recovery, tiredness can last several weeks, then it passes.

# The platelet number

In dengue the platelet count falls in almost everyone, because the body makes fewer platelets and destroys more of them. So a falling count is expected, and it is not by itself a sign that things are going wrong.

The doctor watches more than the count: the warning signs, how much the child drinks, and how they look. A child who is drinking well, passing urine and playing a little tells the doctor a great deal, often more than a single number on a report.

Many families think a low count means platelets must be given. Giving platelets just because the count is low has not been shown to prevent bleeding, and transfusions can cause harm of their own. Doctors give platelets when there is serious bleeding or a high risk of it. If you are worried about the count, ask the doctor what they are watching for, and why.

# Caring for someone with dengue at home

- Rest, and plenty to drink: water, oral rehydration solution, soup, coconut water, whatever the child will take.
- For fever and pain, use the medicine your doctor advises, usually paracetamol, in the amount the doctor tells you.
- Do not give ibuprofen or aspirin. They can make bleeding more likely. If someone in the family takes aspirin or a blood thinner from their doctor, tell that doctor about the dengue rather than stopping it on your own.
- There is no specific medicine for dengue itself. Papaya leaf juice, goat's milk and kiwi are often suggested to raise platelets. Ask the doctor before giving any of them, and never let them take the place of drinks and check-ups.
- Keep every blood test the doctor asks for, and the next visit.

Dengue spreads through mosquito bites. The mosquitoes that spread it bite during the day, so use a net for daytime sleep, and empty and clean water containers every week.

# How the story goes on

The doctor explains that the count was expected to fall, and that the next two days matter more than the number. The family stops sending reports to the group and starts watching the girl instead: how much she drinks, whether she passes urine, whether her stomach hurts.

She has no warning signs. Her count turns around on its own and climbs over the following days, and she needs no transfusion. She is tired for a few weeks, then back at school. The aunt still credits the papaya leaf.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- severe stomach pain, or vomiting again and again
- bleeding from the gums or nose, blood in vomit, stool or urine, or new bruises
- fast breathing
- the person is very tired, weak or restless
- cold, pale skin, or great thirst

These can come during the fever or soon after it goes.

# Take these questions to your doctor

- Which day of the illness is this, and when is the risky time for us?
- What should we watch for at home, and when should we come back?
- How much should my child drink in a day?
- Which medicine can we give for fever, and which should we avoid?
- Does my child need a platelet transfusion, and why or why not?

# Common questions

## When is dengue most dangerous?

Often just after the fever goes away. That is when the warning signs need the closest watching.

## Does papaya leaf juice raise platelets?

The World Health Organization says there is no specific treatment for dengue. Ask the doctor before giving papaya leaf juice, and never let it replace drinks or check-ups.

## Does every dengue patient with low platelets need a platelet transfusion?

No. A falling count is expected in dengue. Doctors give platelets when there is serious bleeding or a high risk of it, not for the number alone.
