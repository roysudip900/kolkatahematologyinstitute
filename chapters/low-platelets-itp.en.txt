# What is going on in ITP

Platelets are the tiny cells that plug a cut. In ITP, the body's defence system, the immune system, attacks its own platelets by mistake, and the body may also make fewer of them. Why this happens is not known. An infection can set it off.

There is no single quick test for it. The doctor looks at the whole report and at the platelets under the microscope, and sometimes needs a bone marrow test to be sure it is ITP and not another cause.

In children ITP is usually short lived. In adults it more often lasts a year or longer. This long-lasting kind is two to three times more common in women than in men.

# The little girl's ITP often settles

For most children, ITP is not a serious illness. It often goes away within a few weeks or months and does not come back. Her hematologist plans her care and how often to check the count.

While the count is low, keep the dates for repeat counts, know the warning signs, and keep her away from rough games. Ask the doctor which games and sports are safe.

No tonic, juice or milk has to be added. If the family wants to give anything, a herbal remedy included, show it to the doctor first. Be careful with painkillers. Aspirin and ibuprofen can make bleeding worse, and many medicines from the chemist contain them. Ask the doctor which one to give.

# The teacher's ITP may last

Her heavy periods and bleeding gums were signs of the low count. Losing blood every month can also lower the hemoglobin, so she may be tired and pale as well.

ITP that lasts a year or more mostly affects adults. It varies from person to person and can go on for years. Even so, most people with long-lasting ITP can stop treatment at some point and keep a healthy count, and people with serious forms still live for decades.

She can expect regular blood tests and a plan that changes with her count.

# What treatment can do

The doctor chooses from a few kinds of treatment:

- Steroid medicines, often the first one tried. They calm the defence system so the count rises. They have side effects, especially when taken for a long time, so the doctor watches closely.
- A drip of immunoglobulin, which can raise the count and is also used for serious bleeding.
- Medicines that help the body make more platelets.
- Other medicines that stop the defence system destroying platelets.
- Sometimes an operation to remove the spleen, which raises the count but brings a higher risk of infection, so vaccines are given and any fever needs a doctor.
- Platelet transfusion, kept for serious bleeding, because the extra platelets last only a short time.

Some people get worse when a medicine is stopped. So a dose is lowered or stopped only by the doctor, even when the count looks good.

## Steroids without a hematologist

Some people buy steroids from the chemist, or go back to an old prescription, without asking their hematologist. This is dangerous. Steroids do not suit everyone, and how much to take, for how long, and how to come off them is planned for each person.

Taken without a doctor watching, especially at a high dose or for a long time, steroids can cause:

- infections that come more often and can be serious
- high blood sugar or diabetes
- high blood pressure
- weak, thinning bones
- mood changes, low mood, anxiety and poor sleep
- weight gain and a round, puffy face
- eye problems such as cataract and glaucoma
- indigestion and heartburn
- slow growth in children

Stopping them suddenly after a while can be dangerous too, because the body may not make enough of its own steroid hormone for a time. If you have been taking steroids on your own, tell your hematologist now, and ask before you stop them.

# When a baby is planned

Pregnancy can make ITP worse, so the doctor wants to know before you plan a baby. During pregnancy the count is checked often, by a hematologist together with a doctor for high-risk pregnancies. If the count is very low near the end, treatment can raise it before delivery to lower the risk of bleeding. The baby may have a low count at birth, and most of the time it is normal within a few days.

# How the stories go on

The little girl's mother keeps every appointment and gives only what the hematologist prescribes, and the grandmother is talked out of the tonic. The spots fade over the following weeks. The count climbs back, and the doctor spaces out the visits.

The teacher's ITP is still there after a year. She takes the medicine her hematologist chooses, sees a gynaecologist for the heavy periods, and has her iron checked. When she and her husband are ready, she tells both doctors first, and her pregnancy is planned around her count.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- bleeding that does not stop after pressing on it firmly
- blood in vomit, black or bloody stool, or blood in the urine
- a sudden severe headache, vomiting, unusual sleepiness or confusion, especially after a fall or a knock on the head
- a period so heavy that you feel faint or dizzy
- a fever, if the spleen has been removed
- vomiting, severe weakness, dizziness or fainting while taking steroids, or soon after stopping them

# Take these questions to your doctor

A hematologist can tell you which path your ITP is on. Bring every report, and ask:

- Is this ITP, or could it be something else?
- What is the plan for my ITP, and for how long?
- When should the count be checked again?
- Which painkiller and fever medicine are safe?
- Which games, sports or work should we avoid for now?
- If a baby is planned, what needs to happen first?

# Common questions

## Is ITP a type of blood cancer?

No. ITP happens when the immune system destroys platelets by mistake. A low count has many causes, and the doctor looks at the whole report to tell them apart.

## Will my child's ITP come back?

In most children it goes away within a few weeks or months and does not return. A few children have ITP that lasts longer, and their doctor plans the care for that.

## Can a woman with ITP have a baby?

Yes, many do. Tell your hematologist before you plan a pregnancy. Your count will be checked often, and treatment before delivery is possible if it is very low.
