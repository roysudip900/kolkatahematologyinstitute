> A low platelet count means the blood has fewer of the tiny cells that help stop bleeding. A mild drop often causes no trouble and is found by chance, but the cause still needs a doctor, and bleeding that will not stop needs a hospital the same day.

The report had one line printed in bold, with a small L beside it: platelets.

He is forty-five. The blood test was only for an office check-up, and he has never had a nosebleed in his life. That night he typed the number into his phone, and the first page said blood cancer. He did not sleep. In the morning he did not tell his wife.

In another home, a mother is bathing her son, who is five. On both shins she sees tiny red dots, like pinpricks of red ink, and a purple bruise on his knee that nobody can explain. He had a cold two weeks ago. He is playing as usual.

Both want to know how worried they should be.

# What platelets do

Platelets are tiny cells in the blood. When you cut yourself, they rush to the spot and stick together to form a plug that seals the wound.

On most reports, the normal range for adults is between 1.5 lakh and 4.5 lakh (150,000 to 450,000). Below that is low. Labs print their own range next to the result, so read it from your report.

A mild drop often causes no trouble at all, and many people find out only from a routine blood test. Serious bleeding usually happens only when the count is very low. A fully normal count is not needed to stay safe from serious bleeding.

# Why the count falls

There are three ways it happens. The bone marrow, where blood is made, makes too few. Or the body destroys or uses up the platelets faster than they are made. Or a large spleen holds on to too many of them.

The common reasons a doctor thinks of first:

- A fever from an infection. Viral and bacterial infections can lower the count for a while. In Bengal, dengue is the one everyone thinks of in the monsoon, and it has its own part.
- ITP. The body's own defence system destroys platelets by mistake. In children it sometimes follows a viral illness.
- Medicines. Some medicines lower the count, or set off a reaction that destroys platelets.
- Alcohol, and a large spleen.
- Late pregnancy. Some women have a mild drop near delivery, and it usually needs no treatment.

Less often, the cause is in the bone marrow itself, as in aplastic anaemia, leukaemia or lymphoma. So a low count always needs a doctor's look, even when you feel well.

Now and then the count is falsely low because the platelets clump together in the tube. A repeat test, or a look at the blood under the microscope, sorts this out.

# Spots, bruises and bleeding

Many people with a low count notice nothing. When there are signs, they come from bleeding:

- tiny flat red dots under the skin, often on the shins, that look like a rash
- purple or brownish patches, or bruises that come easily
- nosebleeds, or bleeding from the gums
- a cut that keeps bleeding for a long time
- blood in the urine or stool, or periods much heavier than usual

# What the doctor will do

The doctor asks about recent fevers, every medicine you take (including anything bought from the chemist), and alcohol. They examine you, feel for the spleen, and look at the rest of the report: the hemoglobin and the white cells matter as much as the platelets.

Usually the count is repeated, and a drop of blood is looked at under the microscope. The smear and the other counts usually show the doctor which way to look. Some people need tests for infections, and a few need a bone marrow test.

# ITP, in a few lines

In ITP the defence system attacks the body's own platelets. In children it often goes away by itself within a few weeks or months. In adults it more often lasts. Many people with mild ITP need no treatment, only regular checks. Others need medicines that calm the defence system or help the body make more platelets, and sometimes an operation to remove the spleen. The doctor decides, and ITP will have its own part.

# While the count is low

- Painkillers. Aspirin and ibuprofen can make bleeding worse, and many medicines sold for pain and fever contain them. Ask the doctor before taking any painkiller from the chemist. If a doctor has prescribed aspirin or a blood thinner for your heart, do not stop it on your own: tell that doctor about the low count straight away.
- Rough play and contact sports, such as football and wrestling, can cause bleeding. Ask the doctor what is safe for now.
- Alcohol can lower the count further.

# How the stories go on

The man shows the report to a hematologist. The repeat count is a little low but steady, the smear and the other counts look normal, and he takes no painkillers or alcohol. The doctor asks to check it again in a few weeks. He tells his wife that evening.

The boy's count is very low, and the doctor thinks it is ITP after his cold. He has no bleeding apart from the spots, so for now he is watched closely, kept away from rough games, and his mother knows exactly when to bring him in.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- bleeding that does not stop after pressing on it firmly
- blood in vomit, black or bloody stool, or blood in urine
- a sudden severe headache, or unusual drowsiness or confusion
- during or just after a fever: severe stomach pain, vomiting again and again, bleeding gums or nose, restlessness, or cold pale skin. These can come as the fever goes down.

# Take these questions to your doctor

Bring every report you have, old and new, and ask:

- What is my count, and is it going up or down?
- What do you think is causing it?
- Which painkiller is safe for me to take?
- Is there anything I or my child should avoid for now?
- When should the count be checked again?

# Common questions

## What is a normal platelet count?

For adults, most labs give a range of about 1.5 lakh to 4.5 lakh. Your report shows the range that lab uses.

## Is a low platelet count always serious?

No. A mild drop is often found by chance and causes no trouble. But the cause still needs finding, so show the report to a doctor.

## Does a low platelet count mean blood cancer?

Blood cancers are one of many causes. Infections, medicines, ITP and a large spleen can all lower the count. The smear and the other counts on the report help the doctor tell them apart.
