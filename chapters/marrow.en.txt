> The bone marrow test takes a small sample of the soft marrow from the back of the hip bone, with the skin numbed, so that a pathologist can see how blood is being made. It is done from the hip, not the spine, the body replaces the small sample, and it often answers what a blood report alone cannot, so ask your hematologist why you need it and what it will show.

# What the marrow test is

Bone marrow is the soft tissue inside the bones, where red cells, white cells and platelets are made. The blood report counts the cells that have come out into the blood. The marrow test looks at the place where they are made.

There are two parts, usually done together, from the back of the hip bone. The first, the aspirate, draws out a small amount of liquid marrow through a hollow needle. The second, the biopsy, takes a thin core of marrow and bone in one piece. A pathologist then looks at both under a microscope.

# Why it may be needed

The marrow test is asked for when the blood report shows a problem but not its cause. It can show:

- why the blood counts are low or high
- whether a blood disease is there, and which type
- how far some blood diseases have developed
- how well treatment is working

Special tests can also be done on the same sample, such as tests on the chromosomes of the cells. These help the hematologist confirm the type of illness and plan treatment.

# What happens on the day

You lie down, and the skin over the back of the hip bone is cleaned and numbed with a local anaesthetic. Some people are also given medicine to help them relax. Young children may be given a general anaesthetic, so they sleep through it.

The doctor puts a hollow needle through the numbed skin into the bone and draws out the liquid marrow, then takes the small core. A dressing goes on, and you rest for a while. The spot can feel stiff or sore for a few days. Like any needle test, it can sometimes cause bleeding or an infection at the spot.

Before the test, tell the doctor about any medicines you take, especially blood thinners, and about any allergies. The doctor decides what to do about them.

Do not start tonics, iron or any other medicine because of a blood report, and do not stop a medicine you take, such as a blood thinner, on your own. Show the report to the hematologist first.

# Will the test leave him weak?

The marrow is not the spinal cord. The test is done from the hip bone, and it does not touch the spine.

The sample is small, and the marrow keeps making new cells. Even people who donate marrow for a transplant get it back, as their body replaces what they gave. The tiredness he feels comes from his low counts, and the test is how the hematologist finds the reason.

# What the pathologist looks at

The pathologist looks at the liquid sample to count the different kinds of young cells and see their shape. The core shows how full the marrow is and how it is built. A first look at the liquid sample can sometimes be ready quickly. The core takes longer, because the bone in it has to be softened before it can be cut into thin slices for the microscope.

The pathologist and the hematologist then put the answers together with the blood report and the person's signs.

# How the stories go on

The teacher's wife asks the hematologist to explain the test to him again, with his son on the phone. He has the test from his hip, and the next week he is back at the tea stall arguing about football. In his case, the report shows why his counts are low, and his treatment begins.

The girl has her test while she sleeps, and wakes up asking for an ice cream. Her mother keeps the report in a plastic folder for the next visit.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

After a marrow test:

- the spot keeps bleeding after you press on it for a while
- you get a fever or shivering
- the spot becomes red, hot, swollen, or more painful each day

# Take these questions to your doctor

Ask the hematologist:

- Why do I need the marrow test, and what will it show?
- Will it be done from the hip, and will I be awake?
- What about my blood thinner or other medicines before the test?
- Can someone come with me, and when can I go home?
- When will the report be ready, and who will explain it to me?

# Common questions

## Does the bone marrow test hurt?

The skin is numbed with a local anaesthetic first, and some people are given medicine to help them relax. Young children may be given a general anaesthetic. The spot can feel sore for a few days afterwards.

## Is the marrow taken from the spine?

No. It is usually taken from the back of the hip bone. Bone marrow is the soft tissue inside the bones, and it is different from the spinal cord.

## Will the test make me weak?

The sample is small, and the body keeps making new marrow cells. Tiredness can come from the illness that the test is looking for.
