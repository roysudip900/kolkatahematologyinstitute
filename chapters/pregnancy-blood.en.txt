> Pregnancy changes the blood. Anaemia is the most common problem, a slightly low platelet count is common and usually harmless, a carrier test can show whether a baby could have thalassemia or sickle cell disease, and the chance of a blood clot rises in pregnancy and the weeks after birth. Most of these can be found early with the blood tests at pregnancy check-ups and treated, so keep every check-up and show the reports to your doctor.

# Why pregnancy changes the blood

During pregnancy the body makes more blood to support the baby's growth. It needs more iron and other nutrients to do this. If it does not get enough, anaemia follows.

The chance of a blood clot in a vein also rises during pregnancy and for six weeks after the birth.

# Anaemia in pregnancy

Anaemia, mostly from too little iron, is common in pregnancy. It can make a woman feel weak, tired or breathless. Serious or untreated anaemia can lead to an early birth, more blood loss during delivery, a baby with a low birth weight, and anaemia in the baby.

## Tests

Under the Anemia Mukt Bharat programme, haemoglobin is tested at every pregnancy check-up, at least four times in a pregnancy, and more often in a high-risk pregnancy. The doctor tells you whether your haemoglobin is adequate.

## Prevention

- Before pregnancy and in the first three months, folic acid tablets lower the chance of a problem with the baby's brain or spine. Women with sickle cell disease or thalassemia need a higher amount, which the doctor prescribes.
- From the second three months of pregnancy, the programme gives red iron and folic acid tablets, and continues them for some months after the birth.
- Eat iron-rich local foods, with foods that have vitamin C, which helps the body take in iron.

## Treatment

- Mild or moderate anaemia is treated with more iron and folic acid tablets. The haemoglobin is checked again after a month.
- If the tablets upset the stomach, tell the ASHA, the nurse or the doctor. Do not just stop them. The doctor can give iron another way.
- For moderate anaemia, especially if tablets cannot be taken, iron can be given through a drip into a vein, after the first three months of pregnancy.
- Severe anaemia needs a hospital, for tests, iron through a drip or a blood transfusion.
- If the haemoglobin does not rise even after taking iron as advised, the woman is sent to a bigger hospital to look for another cause. More of the same iron will not fix it.

# Low platelets in pregnancy

Platelets help the blood clot. In pregnancy, the platelet count is often a little lower than usual. This is called gestational thrombocytopenia. It is common, usually causes no problem for the mother or the baby, and the count usually comes back after the birth. Most women need no treatment.

Sometimes a low count has another cause that needs care:

- pre-eclampsia, a condition with high blood pressure in pregnancy, and a severe form of it called HELLP syndrome
- immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), which can get worse during pregnancy
- some rarer blood conditions

A woman with a very low count may see a hematologist and a doctor for high-risk pregnancies. She may need treatment to raise the count before delivery, to prevent heavy bleeding. Her baby may also have a low count at birth, which usually comes back within a few days.

# The carrier test for thalassemia and sickle cell

A carrier has one changed gene for thalassemia or sickle cell disease and is usually well. If both parents are carriers, each pregnancy has a chance of a baby with the disease.

- A blood test shows whether the mother is a carrier. It is best done early in pregnancy, or before it.
- If the mother is a carrier, the father is offered the test too.
- If both are carriers, a test during pregnancy can show whether the baby has the condition. The team explains the choices and supports the couple in whatever they decide.
- Tell the doctor early if anyone in either family has thalassemia or sickle cell disease.

The Thalassemia and Sickle cell disease chapters on this site explain how these run in families.

# Blood clots in pregnancy

The chance of a clot in a deep vein (DVT) is higher during pregnancy and in the six weeks after having a baby. A clot in the leg shows as pain and swelling in one leg, often the calf. If a piece travels to the lungs, it causes sudden breathlessness or chest pain.

- Warfarin and the newer blood-thinning tablets are not used in pregnancy.
- Heparin injections can be used in pregnancy. A woman who gets a clot in pregnancy usually needs them for the rest of the pregnancy and until the baby is six weeks old. The doctor decides how much.
- A woman who already takes a blood thinner and is planning a baby, or finds she is pregnant, should speak to her doctor soon. The doctor plans any change.

The Blood clots chapter on this site explains more.

# If you already have a blood condition

Sickle cell disease, thalassemia, ITP or a past clot all need planning before pregnancy. See the hematologist before trying for a baby, if you can, and early in pregnancy if not. Some medicines are not used in pregnancy, such as hydroxyurea for sickle cell disease, so the doctor plans the change. Do not stop or change a medicine on your own.

# Go to a hospital now if

Go at once, and do not drive yourself, if you are pregnant or have had a baby in the last few weeks and you have:

- heavy bleeding from the vagina
- sudden breathlessness, chest pain, or coughing up blood
- pain and swelling in one leg
- a severe headache that does not go away, blurred vision or flashing lights, pain below the ribs, or sudden swelling of the face, hands or feet
- bleeding from the gums or nose that does not stop, or many bruises without injury
- feeling very weak, faint, or a racing heartbeat
- a baby that moves less than usual, or fluid leaking

# Take these questions to your doctor

- Is my haemoglobin adequate for this stage of pregnancy?
- Which iron and folic acid tablets should I take, and for how long?
- What should I do if the tablets upset my stomach?
- Do I need iron through a drip?
- Is my platelet count a worry, and will it be checked again?
- Should my husband and I have the carrier test?
- Do I have a higher chance of a clot, and do I need anything to lower it?

# Common questions

## Is feeling tired in pregnancy a worry?

Some tiredness is common, but tiredness with breathlessness, a fast heartbeat or a pale face can be anaemia. Ask for your haemoglobin to be checked.

## Will a low platelet count harm my baby?

A slightly low count in pregnancy is common and usually harmless for mother and baby. The doctor checks the cause and keeps an eye on the count.

## My husband and I are both thalassemia carriers. What now?

Each pregnancy has a chance of a baby with thalassemia major. A test during pregnancy can show whether the baby has it. Talk to your doctor early.
