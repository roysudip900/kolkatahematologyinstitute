> Sickle cell disease is an inherited condition in which red blood cells become stiff and curved like a sickle, block small blood vessels and break down early, causing pain, tiredness and infections. # What sickle cell disease is

Red blood cells are usually round and soft, and slip easily through small blood vessels. In sickle cell disease, they can become stiff and curved, like a sickle. These cells get stuck in small vessels and block the blood flow, which causes sudden pain, and they break down early, which causes anaemia and tiredness.

It is passed on in families. A child has sickle cell disease when the gene comes from both parents. A person who gets the gene from only one parent is a carrier. Carriers usually stay well, but two carriers can have a child with the disease.

In India it is found more in some communities, including some tribal communities in eastern and central India.

# Signs of sickle cell disease

Signs can start from about six months of age. They come and go, and many people feel well between them.

- pain in the bones, joints, muscles, back or chest
- painful swelling of the hands and feet, especially in small children
- tiredness and weakness, from anaemia
- infections more often
- tummy pain, or breathing problems

A blood test confirms it, and can also show who in the family is a carrier.

# A sickle cell crisis

A crisis is a time of sudden, severe pain, sometimes with fever or other symptoms. It can last hours, days or weeks. Things that can set one off include not drinking enough water, sudden changes between heat and cold, and stress.

# What treatment can do

Sickle cell disease usually lasts for life, but treatment helps control the symptoms and prevent problems. A hematologist leads a care team, which can include doctors for children, pain and emergencies, a counsellor, nurses and a physiotherapist. The team writes a treatment plan with the family, with regular check-ups. Sticking with the plan lowers the risk of complications.

## Everyday care to prevent crises

- Drink plenty of water every day, more in hot weather and when ill.
- Avoid sudden changes in temperature. Dress warmly in cold weather, and ease slowly into cold water instead of jumping in.
- Gentle to moderate exercise, such as walking or yoga, is good. Very hard exercise can affect breathing.
- Do not smoke, and avoid alcohol. They raise the risk of a serious lung problem called acute chest syndrome.
- Rest when tired, and find ways to manage stress, such as breathing exercises.

## A pain crisis at home

- The team gives each family a written pain plan: which medicines help this person, what to give at home, and when to come to hospital. Keep it with you and show it at any hospital.
- Mild to moderate pain can often be treated at home with painkillers the team advises, such as paracetamol or ibuprofen. Ibuprofen and similar painkillers can harm the kidneys and stomach in some people, so ask the team which ones are safe for you.
- Drink water, keep warm, and put warmth on the painful area.
- Massage, yoga, relaxation guided by an audio or video recording, and virtual reality games can ease pain alongside the medicines.

## A pain crisis in hospital

- Severe pain, or pain that the home plan does not settle, needs hospital. The American Society of Hematology advises that pain medicine is given within an hour of arriving, and that the pain is checked again often until it is under control.
- Strong painkillers called opioids, such as morphine, are often needed. The doctor chooses the amount from what has worked for this person before. They can be given as an injection under the skin, through a vein, or by a pump that the patient presses when the pain comes.
- An anti-inflammatory painkiller may be added for a few days, if the kidneys and stomach allow.
- Steroid medicines are not used to treat the pain itself.
- A drip is given if the body is short of water.
- When the pain still does not settle, some hospitals with the right experts add ketamine through a drip, or numb the painful area with a nerve block.

## Pain that lasts

Some teenagers and adults have pain on most days for months. The American Society of Hematology estimates this happens to thirty to forty in every hundred teenagers and adults with sickle cell disease. Treatment combines several things:

- counselling that teaches ways to cope with pain, called cognitive behavioural therapy, and relaxation or mindfulness
- massage or acupuncture, if they suit the person
- for adults, medicines that calm pain nerves, such as duloxetine, amitriptyline or pregabalin
- when the hip bone is damaged by poor blood supply, called avascular necrosis, duloxetine or an anti-inflammatory painkiller may help adults
- long-term strong painkillers only when other treatments have not worked, with close check-ups, and no alcohol or sleeping pills alongside them

Long pain often comes with worry and low mood. The team checks for depression and anxiety and treats them too.

## Preventing infections

People with sickle cell disease get infections more easily, and some can be serious.

- Children are usually given an antibiotic, penicillin, to lower the risk of a serious blood infection. Many doctors stop it at about five years of age, and some continue it for life. People whose spleen has been removed keep taking it. The doctor decides how long.
- Vaccines matter: the routine childhood vaccines, the pneumonia and meningitis vaccines, and the flu vaccine. Ask the team which ones are due.
- Any fever needs a call to the doctor the same day.

## Medicines that change the disease

- Hydroxyurea (also called hydroxycarbamide) is the main one. It is a tablet that reduces sickling. It lowers the number of pain crises, acute chest syndrome, transfusions and hospital stays, can improve anaemia, and may help people live longer. Doctors usually start it in babies from about nine months of age. For some children at high risk of stroke, it can take the place of regular transfusions after a time.
- Hydroxyurea can lower the white cells or platelets, and rarely makes anaemia worse, so the team checks the blood counts regularly. These effects usually settle when the doctor pauses it. It is not taken in pregnancy. Do not stop it on your own.
- L-glutamine is a powder mixed into a drink or food, for people from five years of age. It can mean fewer pain crises, fewer hospital stays and less acute chest syndrome.
- Crizanlizumab is given through a drip into a vein in hospital, for people from sixteen years of age. It helps stop blood cells sticking to the vessel walls, and prevents pain crises. The team watches the person during and after the drip.
- One medicine, voxelotor, was withdrawn in 2024 because its benefits did not outweigh its risks.

Regular transfusions are not the first choice just to prevent repeated pain crises. Hydroxyurea and the other medicines come first.

## Blood transfusions

Transfusions raise the number of healthy red cells, which bend easily and do not sickle.

- A transfusion is given quickly for serious anaemia, a stroke or acute chest syndrome, and usually before an operation.
- Regular transfusions help prevent another stroke after a first one, and prevent a first stroke in children whose head scan shows a high risk. Some people need them in pregnancy, or when hydroxyurea alone is not enough.
- A transfusion takes one to four hours. People with sickle cell disease need specially matched blood.
- Some people need a red cell exchange, in which their blood is swapped for donated blood.
- Transfusions can bring problems: the body can react against donated blood, infections can pass on, and iron builds up. People who need many transfusions may need medicine to remove the extra iron.

## Treatments that can cure

A stem cell or bone marrow transplant can cure sickle cell disease. It needs a well-matched donor, usually a close blood relative. Most transplants are done in children who have had complications such as a stroke, chest crises or repeated pain crises. In children with a matched relative as donor, the transplant works in about ninety in every hundred. It is riskier in adults.

The risks are serious: the donor's cells attacking the body (graft-versus-host disease), serious infections, fits, not being able to have children, some cancers later in life, and the transplant failing. About five in every hundred people under sixteen, and about nine in every hundred older people, have died from the transplant. Ask about saving eggs or sperm before a transplant.

Gene therapy uses the person's own stem cells. They are collected from the blood and changed in a laboratory, so that one kind changes a gene already there and the other adds a new gene. Chemotherapy then makes space in the marrow, and the changed cells are given back through a vein. Two gene therapies were approved in the United States in December 2023.

Transplants and gene therapies need weeks in hospital, are offered at only a few centres and cost a lot, but are given once. After either one, a person can still pass the sickle cell gene to their children.

## Regular check-ups

Sickle cell disease raises the risk of stroke, eye problems and damage to the lungs, heart and kidneys. Regular check-ups find these early. Children may have an ultrasound scan of the head to check the stroke risk. Parents can learn from the team how to feel a child's spleen, because a sudden swelling of the spleen is an emergency.

## Family planning

A blood test can show whether a partner is a carrier. If both partners are carriers, each pregnancy has a chance of a child with sickle cell disease, and tests can be done during pregnancy. Women with sickle cell disease can have children, with extra care from the team during pregnancy.

# Go to a hospital now if

Go at once, and do not drive yourself, if a person with sickle cell disease has:

- a fever
- severe pain that does not settle with the home plan, or pain with vomiting
- chest pain, a cough or difficulty breathing
- a tummy that suddenly swells, especially in a child, or a child who becomes very pale and weak
- sudden weakness of the face, an arm or a leg, trouble speaking, or a fit
- an erection that is painful or does not go away

# Take these questions to your doctor

- What is our plan for pain at home, and when should we come to hospital?
- Which vaccines and antibiotics does my child need, and for how long?
- Is hydroxyurea right for us?
- Which check-ups do we need, and how often?
- Should other family members, and a future partner, be tested?
- Is a transplant or gene therapy possible for us?

# Common questions

## Is sickle cell disease the same as being a carrier?

No. A carrier has the gene from one parent and usually stays well. The disease comes when the gene comes from both parents.

## Can sickle cell disease be cured?

A transplant or gene therapy can cure it in some people, but they are not suitable for everyone. For most people, treatment and good everyday care prevent many crises and complications.

## Can a person with sickle cell disease marry and have children?

Yes. A partner can have a blood test to see if they are a carrier, and the team can explain the chances for each pregnancy and help plan care.
