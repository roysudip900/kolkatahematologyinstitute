Treatment is aimed at that cause, and the spleen is removed only in some cases. An enlarged spleen can burst after a blow to the belly, so pain behind the left ribs with dizziness after an injury needs a hospital at once.

He is forty and has come to Kolkata from his village for work. For months he has had fever on and off, he has lost weight, and he feels full after a few mouthfuls of rice. He thought it was weakness from work, until the doctor told him his spleen was much larger than it should be.

In Bardhaman, a girl of ten has thalassemia major and has blood transfusions. At her check-up the hematologist says her spleen has grown, and her parents hear the word "operation" for the first time.

He wants to know why his spleen has grown. Her parents want to know whether the spleen must come out.

# What the spleen does

The spleen is about the size of a fist. It lies high on the left side of the belly, tucked behind the lower ribs. It:

- fights germs in the blood, because it holds infection-fighting white cells
- controls the level of red cells, white cells and platelets
- filters the blood and removes old or damaged red cells

A person can live without a spleen, because the liver takes over many of its jobs.

# Why the spleen grows

An enlarged spleen is a sign that something else is going on. Causes include:

- infections. In India, one of these is kala-azar (visceral leishmaniasis), which causes fever on and off, weight loss, a large spleen and liver, and anaemia
- liver disease, such as cirrhosis
- blood cancers, such as leukaemia, and myeloproliferative neoplasms such as polycythaemia vera and myelofibrosis
- thalassemia, because the spleen keeps destroying red cells and makes some blood of its own
- rheumatoid arthritis and some other long-term illnesses

The hematologist or physician looks for the cause, because that is what needs treatment.

# Signs

Many people with a large spleen notice nothing. Others notice:

- getting full after only a little food, because the spleen pushes on the stomach
- discomfort or pain behind the left ribs
- tiredness and anaemia
- infections more often
- bleeding or bruising easily

A large spleen can also start removing healthy blood cells, which lowers the red cells, white cells and platelets.

# How it is found

Doctors can often feel an enlarged spleen by pressing on the belly. An ultrasound, CT or MRI scan, and blood tests, confirm it and help find the cause. Other tests depend on what the doctor suspects.

# What treatment can do

- The cause is treated: the infection, the liver disease, the thalassemia or the blood cancer, and the spleen is watched.
- The spleen is not usually removed just because it is enlarged.
- In thalassemia, regular and adequate transfusions can keep the spleen from growing, and a spleen that grew during a time of too little blood can shrink again. Removing the spleen is kept for some patients, because it brings its own risks: serious infections, blood clots and high pressure in the lung vessels.
- Kala-azar can be treated and cured with medicine, and needs treatment early.
- If the spleen must be removed, it is usually done by keyhole surgery. Vaccines are given before the operation when there is time.

While the spleen is enlarged, avoid contact sports and rough play, because a blow can make it burst.

# Living without a spleen

After the spleen is removed, most infections can still be handled, but there is a small, lifelong risk that a serious infection develops very quickly. Young children have a higher risk.

- Have the vaccines the doctor advises, including those against pneumonia, meningitis and flu.
- The doctor may advise antibiotics to prevent infection, sometimes for years. Take them as the doctor says, and do not stop them on your own.
- Tell every doctor and dentist that you have no spleen, and carry a card or medical ID.
- See a doctor the same day for any sign of infection, and urgently after an animal bite.
- People without a spleen can get severe malaria. Use mosquito nets and repellent, and ask the doctor before travelling to a malaria area.

# How the stories go on

The man's blood tests and a test for kala-azar are positive. He is treated with medicine in hospital, his fever settles, and over the following months his spleen shrinks and he eats better.

The girl's hematologist checks that her transfusions are keeping her haemoglobin adequate, adjusts her plan, and watches her spleen with an ultrasound. Surgery is not needed for now, and she is told to skip rough games until her spleen is smaller.

# Go to a hospital now if

Go at once, and do not drive yourself, if:

- after a blow to the belly, a fall or an accident, even days or weeks later, there is pain behind the left ribs, with dizziness or a racing heartbeat
- someone with no spleen has a high fever, shivering, a severe headache, or a headache with drowsiness or a rash
- a child with sickle cell disease suddenly has a swollen belly, or becomes very pale and weak
- there is bleeding that will not stop, or many bruises without injury

# Take these questions to your doctor

- What is making my spleen grow?
- Which tests do I need to find the cause?
- Will the spleen shrink once the cause is treated?
- Should I avoid any sports or work while it is large?
- Is removing the spleen being considered, and what are the risks?
- If the spleen is removed, which vaccines and antibiotics will I need?

# Common questions

## Is an enlarged spleen a cancer?

Not always. It is a sign of many conditions, including infections, liver disease and thalassemia. Some blood cancers can cause it too, so the doctor looks for the cause.

## Can I live without my spleen?

Yes. The liver takes over many of its jobs. There is a small lifelong risk of serious infection, which vaccines, antibiotics the doctor advises and quick care for a fever help lower.

## My child has thalassemia and a big spleen. Must it be removed?

Not always. Adequate regular transfusions can stop it growing and sometimes shrink it. The hematologist considers removal only for some patients, because it has risks of its own.
