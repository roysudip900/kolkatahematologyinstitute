> With good care, many children with thalassemia go to school, play, eat ordinary family food, and grow up to work, marry and have children. Ask your hematologist about school, sport, vaccines and planning a family, and see a doctor the same day if a fever comes.

The boy is ten now. His mother keeps him home on transfusion days, and the day after, to rest. He does not go on school trips or out in the lane when the other boys play. Fish has gone from his plate because someone told her it has iron. When the neighbours ask why he looks pale, she says he has a weak stomach.

In another home, a young woman of twenty-six is getting ready for her engagement. She has had thalassemia major all her life, she works in an office in Salt Lake, and she has never missed a transfusion. Her fiancé knows. His family has only just found out, and his mother has asked one question: can she have children?

Both families are frightened, and neither has asked the hematologist about everyday life.

# A near-normal life is the aim

With regular transfusions and the iron medicine taken well, many people with thalassemia now live close to a normal life. Fear and hiding can hold a child back too, when the child is kept indoors and the family will not say the word.

Nobody catches thalassemia from a child who has it, by touch, by sharing food, or by playing together. Friends and classmates are safe.

# School

A child with thalassemia can get a full education. The biggest problem is the days missed for transfusions. Some centres have evening or weekend slots, so ask whether the transfusion can be given at a time that does not cut into school, and whether the extra day at home is really needed.

Tell the class teacher what they need to know, and that a fever needs a doctor quickly. A teacher who has not been told may worry and hold the child back. Your hematologist can write a short note for the school.

# Play

Running, cycling, cricket and football keep the body strong and help a child feel like everyone else. Before the teens, a child on good care can usually play freely.

As the child grows, the doctor keeps checking the heart and the bones. If your teenager wants to play a sport seriously, ask the hematologist first. The answer depends on your child, and the clinic can check.

# Food

A child on regular transfusions gets far more iron from the blood than from any food. Fish, eggs, meat, dal and rice can stay on the plate, and a child who eats well grows better. If your child needs blood only now and then, the advice on food can be different, so ask the doctor.

Iron tablets and iron tonics have no place in thalassemia, even in pregnancy. If a chemist or a relative suggests one, ask the hematologist first, and if your child already takes one, tell them. Some children need other vitamins or minerals, and the doctor decides that.

# Vaccines

Children with thalassemia need the usual childhood vaccines. Because they receive blood, the hepatitis B vaccine matters even more, and a flu vaccine every year is often advised. A child whose spleen has been removed needs extra vaccines and protection, and a fever in that child is an emergency. It helps if the family is vaccinated too. Ask which vaccines are still missing.

# Growing up

The body may develop later than in friends, and the doctor keeps checking growth. Teenagers start taking charge of their own care step by step, with a parent still checking.

Some teenagers feel angry, low, or tired of being different. Support for the mind is part of thalassemia care, so ask whether a counsellor is available.

# Work and marriage

Many adults with thalassemia work without difficulty. Many marry and have children.

Before marriage, the partner should have a blood test. If the partner also carries thalassemia, each pregnancy has a chance of a baby with thalassemia major, and there are ways to know early in the pregnancy. A pregnancy is planned with the specialist team before it begins, so the heart and the iron can be checked and the medicines reviewed.

# The strain on the family

Years of transfusion days, travel and costs wear parents down. Often the mother carries it alone, and brothers and sisters can feel forgotten.

Share the load. A father, a grandparent or an older sibling can take some transfusion days. Tell the other children what is happening in words they understand. Ask the centre whether you can meet parents of older children with thalassemia.

# How the stories go on

The boy's mother tells the hematologist everything, the fish, the trips, the cricket bat. The doctor moves his transfusion to a Saturday and writes a note for the class teacher. Fish comes back to his plate, and the bat comes down from the cupboard.

The young woman goes to her hematologist with her fiancé. The doctor explains thalassemia to them both, and he gives a blood sample. Whatever it shows, they will plan a pregnancy with the team. His mother still has questions, but now the clinic can answer them.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- fever in a person whose spleen has been removed, or who takes an iron-removing medicine
- breathlessness at rest, chest pain or fainting
- a racing or uneven heartbeat that will not settle
- the person is very pale, drowsy, or too weak to eat or play before the next transfusion is due

# Take these questions to your doctor

- Can my child play every sport, or should we avoid any?
- Can the transfusion be given at a time that does not cut into school?
- Which vaccines has my child had, and which are still missing?
- Is there any food my child should eat more of, or less of?
- Before marriage, what test should my partner have?

# Common questions

## Can a child with thalassemia go to school and play sports?

Usually yes. A child on good care can study and play like other children. The doctor checks the heart and bones as the child grows.

## Should a child with thalassemia stop eating fish or meat?

Usually not, if the child has regular transfusions. The iron from the blood is far more than the iron in food.

## Can a person with thalassemia major marry and have children?

Many do. The partner should be tested before marriage, and the pregnancy is planned with the specialist team.
