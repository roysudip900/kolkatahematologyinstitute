The medicine strips were hidden behind her school certificates, still full.

Her mother found them while cleaning the cupboard before Durga Puja. The girl is sixteen. She has had blood every few weeks all her life, and she has never once missed a transfusion. The iron medicine was another matter. She stopped it quietly some months ago, after a school trip where she did not want her friends to see her take it. Nothing happened. She felt exactly the same. So she never started again.

In another home, a mother sits on the floor every night with a boy of five who clamps his mouth shut. The medicine tastes bad, he cries, and the evening turns into a fight. Some nights his father says, leave it, one night won't matter.

Both mothers are frightened, and both are a little ashamed to tell the doctor.

# Why the medicine cannot take a holiday

Every bag of blood leaves a little iron in the body, and the body has no way of getting rid of it. The iron medicine is the way it leaves. Doctors call it chelation. Taken every day, it aims to take out as much iron as the transfusions bring in.

Iron gives no warning. A child can feel completely well while it settles in the heart, the liver and the glands, and by the time it causes trouble it has usually been there for years. That is why the girl felt the same for months.

The medicine works only when it is taken regularly. Even short breaks can do damage, because the iron from every transfusion keeps arriving. Taking extra later does not make up for missed days, and it can cause harm of its own. If doses have been missed, tell the doctor, who will decide what to do.

# The girl who felt fine

Teenagers often find the daily medicine the hardest part of thalassemia. They want to be like their friends, and they are taking over their own care, often before they are ready for all of it. It happens in many families, and it is nobody's fault.

What helps is sharing the job. The teenager takes charge step by step, and a parent still keeps an eye on it. It helps to find out what got in the way. For this girl it was her friends seeing her take it, and her doctor can work around that.

# The boy who will not open his mouth

For a small child, a fixed time every day helps most: the same moment, after dinner and before brushing teeth, so the medicine becomes part of the evening instead of a fight every night. Many families use a chart on the fridge, with a sticker for each night and a small reward at the end of the week.

Missed nights add up, so one night does matter. If the taste or an upset stomach is the problem, tell the doctor. There is more than one iron medicine, and the doctor can often change the medicine, or the way it is given, to suit the child. Please do not change it or stop it on your own.

# Fever needs a doctor the same day

Some iron medicines can lower the white cells that fight infection. So a fever in a child on the iron medicine needs a doctor and a blood test the same day. Ask your doctor at the start what to do if a fever comes, and keep that plan where everyone at home can see it.

# How the stories go on

The girl's mother does not shout, in the end. They go to the hematologist together, and the girl tells the truth. The doctor checks her iron, they agree on a time she can take the medicine at home before school, and she takes charge of it herself, with her mother checking once a week. The strips run out on time now.

The boy's mother tells the doctor about the nightly fight. His medicine is changed to one he finds easier to take, and it goes in after dinner, before brushing teeth, with a sticker on the fridge for every night. Some nights are still hard, but most go quietly.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- fever in a child who takes an iron-removing medicine, or whose spleen has been removed
- breathlessness at rest, chest pain or fainting
- a racing or uneven heartbeat that will not settle
- your child is very pale, drowsy, or too weak to eat or play before the next transfusion is due

# Take these questions to your doctor

Bring the medicine you have at home, even the unopened strips, to your hematologist, and ask:

- Is the iron in my child's body going up or coming down?
- What should we do if a dose is missed?
- What should we do if a fever comes while my child is on this medicine?
- If the taste or the stomach is the problem, is there another way to give it?
- How can my teenager take charge of it, step by step?

# Common questions

## My child feels fine. Why do they still need the iron medicine?

Because iron gives no warning. It can build up in the heart, the liver and the glands for years while a child feels well. The medicine removes the iron that every transfusion leaves behind.

## Can we skip the iron medicine for a few days?

It is better not to. Iron keeps arriving with every transfusion, and even short breaks can do damage. If something makes the medicine hard to give, tell the doctor, who can often change it.

## Why is fever dangerous with the iron medicine?

Some of these medicines can lower the white cells that fight infection, and a fever may be the first sign. It needs a doctor and a blood test the same day.
