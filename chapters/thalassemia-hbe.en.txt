> Hemoglobin E (HbE) is a change in the hemoglobin gene found across eastern and north-eastern India, and it comes in three forms. HbE trait is harmless and HbE disease is usually mild, but HbE beta thalassemia can range from mild to as serious as thalassemia major, so anyone with it needs a hematologist's care for life.

The report said "HbE disease", and she read the last word three times.

She is thirty and works at a bank. For as long as she can remember, her hemoglobin has been a little low. Doctors have given her iron again and again, and nothing much changed. This time a new test was done, and the word disease was printed on it. That night she wondered how long she had been ill without knowing.

On the other side of the city, a boy of seven is the smallest in his class. When he was four, a hematologist told his parents he had HbE beta thalassemia, a milder type, and that he did not need regular blood. They were so relieved that, after a while, they stopped going back. Last month a fever left him so pale and tired that he needed a blood transfusion. His mother now lies awake wondering whether he will need blood for life, like the neighbour's daughter who has thalassemia major.

The woman wants to know whether she is really ill. The mother wants to know which way her son's illness will go.

# Three names that sound alike

Everyone carries two copies of the instructions for the main part of hemoglobin, one from each parent. HbE is a small change in one copy. It is found across eastern and north-eastern India, including West Bengal, and in some north-eastern areas it is very common.

What it means depends on what the other copy is:

- HbE trait: one copy with E, the other normal. This person is a carrier and is healthy.
- HbE disease: both copies with E. Usually only mildly anaemic, and often found only on a test.
- HbE beta thalassemia: one copy with E, the other with beta thalassemia. This is a form of thalassemia, and it can be mild or severe.

# The woman is not ill in the way she feared

Her HbE disease usually causes nothing more than a slightly low hemoglobin and small red cells. Small red cells can look like a shortage of iron on a report, which is why she was given iron for years. Iron helps only when tests show iron is actually low, and the doctor decides that.

She can work, marry and live a normal life. For the future, her partner's report matters. If he carries HbE or beta thalassemia, they should plan a pregnancy with their doctor. The part on how thalassemia runs in families explains why.

# The boy's thalassemia can change course

HbE beta thalassemia varies a great deal. Two children with what looks like the same report can be very different.

- Some have a mild form, with hemoglobin that stays fairly good. Even so, some of them grow slowly or build up iron later.
- Most are in the middle. They do not need regular transfusions, but an infection can drop the hemoglobin far enough to need blood, and iron can build up over the years.
- Some have a severe form and are treated like thalassemia major, with regular transfusions and medicine to remove iron.

So even when no regular blood is needed, the child still needs regular visits. Iron can build up even without transfusions, and a child's growth, energy and blood count need watching as the years pass. The boy is in the middle group. The fever that sent him to hospital is the kind of event his family needs a plan for.

# What care looks like

Every child and adult with HbE beta thalassemia needs a hematologist, even in the years when nothing seems to be wrong. The visits check the blood count, growth and the iron in the body. The doctor decides if and when blood is needed, and if iron-removing medicine is needed. If the form turns out to be severe, the care is the same as for thalassemia major, and the parts on transfusion and on the iron medicine apply.

The family's part is steady follow-up, a plan for fevers, and every report kept together.

# How the stories go on

The woman takes her old and new reports to a hematologist. The iron tests are normal, so the iron stops for good on the doctor's advice. Before her wedding, her fiancé has his own test, and the two reports are read together.

The boy's parents go back to his hematologist with the discharge papers. He is seen regularly again. His growth is charted, his iron is checked, and his mother knows what to do when the next fever comes.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

For anyone with HbE beta thalassemia:

- a child who is very pale, drowsy, or too weak to eat or play
- breathlessness at rest, chest pain or fainting
- fever, above all if the spleen has been removed or the person takes iron-removing medicine
- fever, shivering, a rash or dark urine during or soon after a transfusion

# Take these questions to your doctor

Bring every report from every lab, old and new, to a hematologist, and ask:

- Is this HbE trait, HbE disease or HbE beta thalassemia?
- Do I, or does my child, need iron, or not?
- How severe is the form, and could that change?
- How often should we come for checks, and what will you check?
- What should we do when a fever comes?
- Should my partner, my brothers and sisters, or my children be tested?

# Common questions

## Is HbE trait a disease?

No. A person with HbE trait is a healthy carrier. It matters only when planning children, if the partner is also a carrier of HbE or beta thalassemia.

## Is HbE disease serious?

Usually not. Most people with HbE disease are only mildly anaemic and live normal lives. A hematologist should still confirm it, because HbE beta thalassemia can look similar on some reports.

## Can a child with HbE beta thalassemia live a normal life?

Many do, with regular care. Because the illness varies and can change, the child needs a hematologist for life, even in the years without transfusions.
