When the baby's report came back, the first person her mother-in-law blamed was her.

Nobody said it in front of the doctor. They said it at home, in the kitchen, in the low voice that families keep for these things. Our side has never had this. It must have come from yours. The young mother said nothing. She had a son of four who had never been ill, and now a daughter of ten months who needed blood. She was half ready to believe it herself.

Across the city, another couple sit with two reports from the same lab. Their wedding is in three months. His report says "HbE trait". Hers says "beta thalassemia trait". The names are different, so they decide the two things have nothing to do with each other. They fold the papers away and talk about the guest list.

# Why it takes two

Each of us carries the instructions for making hemoglobin in two copies, one from our mother and one from our father. Thalassemia comes from a mistake in these instructions.

A person with one faulty copy is a carrier. They are healthy, they need no treatment, and most never find out unless someone tests them. A baby is born with thalassemia major only when both parents are carriers and both pass on the faulty copy. That is why it can stay hidden in a family for generations. Carriers marry people who are not carriers, and nobody falls ill. Then one day two carriers marry.

When both parents are carriers, every pregnancy has a 1 in 4 chance that the baby will have thalassemia major. The chance starts again with each child. It does not matter what happened to the brothers and sisters before.

# The mother did not cause this

Her daughter has thalassemia major, which means the girl received a faulty copy from her mother and another from her father. Neither parent could have known, and neither parent did anything wrong. The father's side has "never had this" only because no one there had married another carrier before, or because no one had been tested.

Her son's good health was no proof of anything either. He was one of the children who did not inherit two faulty copies. He may still be a carrier, and one day, before he marries, he should have the test too.

The same test is worth doing for the baby's aunts, uncles and cousins. A family where one child has thalassemia major almost always has carriers on both sides.

# Two names, one risk

The engaged couple made a mistake that is easy to make in Bengal. HbE and beta thalassemia sound like two separate things, and on their own, each is harmless in a carrier. But they are two faults in the same part of hemoglobin. When a child inherits HbE from one parent and beta thalassemia from the other, the result is a condition called HbE beta thalassemia. Some children with it have mild anaemia. Others need regular blood transfusions, just like thalassemia major.

Only a doctor who reads both reports together can tell a couple where they stand.

# What a couple can do

The best time for the test is before the wedding, or at least before a pregnancy. One simple blood test for each partner settles it.

If only one of you is a carrier, your children will not have thalassemia major, though some of them may be carriers like you.

If you are both carriers, you can still marry and have healthy children. Plan each pregnancy with your doctor. The baby can be tested early in the pregnancy, and your doctor will explain what that involves and what your choices are. These are personal decisions, and nobody should make them for you.

# How the stories go on

The young mother's hematologist tests both parents and explains the result to the whole family at once. Her mother-in-law hears it from the doctor, in the doctor's words, and the blame stops. The family turns to what matters now: regular transfusions for the little girl, and a test for her brother when he is older.

The engaged couple mention their reports to a doctor almost by chance. They learn what HbE and beta thalassemia mean together. They still get married. When they plan their first child, they plan it with their doctor, and they know exactly what to ask.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- a baby or young child becomes very pale, too tired to feed, or unusually drowsy
- a child with thalassemia has a fever, especially if the spleen has been removed
- a child with thalassemia is breathless at rest, or faints
- a child has fever, shivering, a rash or dark urine during or soon after a blood transfusion

# Take these questions to your doctor

If thalassemia, HbE or "trait" appears on any report in your family, bring all the reports together to a hematologist, and ask:

- Am I a carrier? Is my partner?
- What do our two reports mean together, for our children?
- Who else in the family should be tested?
- When in a pregnancy can the baby be tested, and what does the test involve?
- If our child is affected, what care will they need?

# Common questions

## If only one parent is a thalassemia carrier, can the child have thalassemia?

The child will not have thalassemia major. A child is born with it only when both parents pass on the faulty gene. The child may be a carrier, which is harmless for the child but worth knowing before they marry.

## Can thalassemia skip a generation?

It can seem to. Carriers are healthy and can pass the gene on for generations without anyone falling ill. Thalassemia major appears only when two carriers have a child together.

## Is HbE trait the same as thalassemia trait?

No, but they are related. Each is harmless in a carrier. If one partner has HbE trait and the other has beta thalassemia trait, their child can be born with HbE beta thalassemia, so both reports should be read together by a doctor.
