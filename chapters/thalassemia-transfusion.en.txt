On transfusion days, the alarm goes off at five.

His mother packs a tiffin box, the file of reports, and the charger for the phone he will play games on all day. They take the first local train into the city. He is seven, and he knows the day care ward better than his school: the bed by the window, the nurse who calls him "hero", the bag of dark red blood hanging above his arm. By evening he is pink in the cheeks again. The next afternoon he is the loudest boy in the football game.

In another family, the father has made a different decision. His daughter's exams fall in the week of her transfusion, and she looks well enough, so he pushes it back until after the exams. Then a relative's wedding comes, and it gets pushed back again. She doesn't complain. She just gets quieter, and slower on the stairs.

# Why the blood has to come on time

A child with thalassemia major cannot make enough working hemoglobin, so the transfusion gives the child what the body cannot. But the blood does a second job that nobody can see. When there is enough of it, the bone marrow, the factory inside the bones, can rest.

When the blood runs low, the factory goes into overdrive. It tries to make red cells it cannot finish. Over months and years the marrow spreads and widens the bones, especially of the face and skull. The spleen grows large and heavy. Growth slows. And a body short of blood starts taking extra iron from food, on top of the iron that every transfusion already leaves behind.

[figure: transfusion-timing]

That is why doctors plan transfusions by the calendar and by a blood test before each one, not by how pale the child looks. By the time a child looks pale, the marrow has been working too hard for a while. The gap between transfusions, and how much blood each time, is worked out for each child, and it changes as they grow.

# Safe blood

Good centres use blood from voluntary donors, test it for infections, and match it to the child before every transfusion. The chance of picking up an infection this way is very low.

Two things help families keep it safe. Keep a record of every transfusion, with the dates and any reaction. And whenever possible, go to the same centre, where the staff know your child and have the records. Mistakes with blood are more likely in a hospital that has never seen your child before.

# What the nurse watches for

During the transfusion, the nurse checks the child's pulse, temperature and blood pressure. Tell the nurse at once if your child feels hot or cold or starts to shiver, gets a rash or itching, has trouble breathing, or complains of pain in the back. Most reactions are mild and easy to treat when they are caught early. Serious ones are rare.

# The iron the blood leaves behind

Every bag of blood leaves a little iron in the body, and the body has no way of getting rid of it. That is why children on regular transfusions also need a medicine that removes iron. Your hematologist will plan both together.

# How the stories go on

The boy keeps his transfusion days. His mother finds that the ward is easier when they arrive early and bring the old file every time. On the train home he sleeps with his head on her arm.

The girl's father brings her in at last, worried about how tired she has become. Her hematologist does not scold him. She explains the factory inside the bones and the spleen that has started to swell, and they agree on dates for the whole term, with the exams in mind. The father writes them on the kitchen calendar.

# Go to a hospital the same day if

- after a transfusion, your child has a high temperature, or feels hot, cold or shivery
- the urine turns very dark, there is blood in it, or your child is passing very little urine
- your child has pain in the lower back, or trouble breathing
- your child is very pale, drowsy or too weak to eat or play before the next transfusion is due

Tell the doctors that your child recently had a transfusion.

# Take these questions to your doctor

If your child needs regular blood, these questions are worth asking at your next visit to the hematologist:

- How will you decide when my child needs the next transfusion?
- What do the blood tests before each transfusion show?
- Is the blood my child gets tested and matched every time?
- What should I watch for after we go home?
- How are you checking the iron that the transfusions leave behind?

# Common questions

## How often does a child with thalassemia major need blood?

Every few weeks. The doctor sets the gap for each child, using a blood test before each transfusion, and changes it as the child grows. Waiting until the child looks pale is too late.

## Does a blood transfusion hurt?

Only the prick when a thin tube is put into a vein in the arm. After that, the blood drips in slowly over a few hours, and most children read, play or sleep through it.

## Is donated blood safe for my child?

Blood transfusion is very safe. Donated blood is tested for infections and matched to your child. Going to the same centre each time, with your child's records, makes it safer still.
