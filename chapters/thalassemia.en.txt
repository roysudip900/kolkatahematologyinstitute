The most important thing to know first: thalassemia is serious, but it is a condition people live with. With regular care, children with even the most severe type now grow up, study, work and marry. The outlook today is very different from what your parents or grandparents may remember.

# What is thalassemia

Inside your red blood cells is hemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen around the body. Hemoglobin is made of two kinds of chains, called alpha and beta. In thalassemia, a fault in a gene means the body makes too little of one of these chains. The red cells that result are small, weak and break down early, so the body runs short of hemoglobin. That shortage is anaemia.

There are two main families. In alpha thalassemia the alpha chains are affected, and in beta thalassemia the beta chains. In India and in Bengal, the type most families meet is beta thalassemia. Bengal also has many people with hemoglobin E, a related change in the beta chain. When a child inherits hemoglobin E from one parent and beta thalassemia from the other, the result is called HbE beta thalassemia, which can be mild or severe.

Doctors group thalassemia by how much it affects daily life:

- Thalassemia trait, or carrier. One faulty gene. The person is healthy and usually needs no treatment. Their red cells are small, and their hemoglobin may be a little low.
- Thalassemia major, also called transfusion dependent thalassemia. Two faulty genes, with very little working beta chain. Symptoms appear within the first 2 years of life, and the child needs regular blood transfusions to live and grow.
- Thalassemia intermedia, also called non transfusion dependent thalassemia. Somewhere in between. Anaemia is mild to moderate. Some people need blood only now and then, for example during an illness, pregnancy or a growth spurt.

A child with thalassemia major often first comes to a doctor between a few months and two years of age, looking pale, feeding poorly, failing to gain weight, sometimes with a swollen tummy because the spleen and liver are enlarged.

# How it runs in families

Thalassemia is not caught from anyone. It does not spread by touch, by food or by living together. It is inherited, passed from parents to children through genes, the way eye colour or height is.

Everyone has two copies of the beta globin gene, one from the mother and one from the father. A person with one faulty copy is a carrier. A child is born with thalassemia major only when both parents pass on a faulty copy.

When both parents are carriers, in every pregnancy:

- there is a 1 in 4 chance the child will have thalassemia major
- a 2 in 4 chance the child will be a carrier like the parents
- a 1 in 4 chance the child will have neither

These chances are the same for every pregnancy. Having one affected child does not mean the next three children will be safe.

Carriers usually do not know they are carriers, because they feel well. A simple blood test, a CBC followed by a test called HPLC or hemoglobin electrophoresis, tells you. The best time to test is before marriage, or at least before pregnancy. If both partners turn out to be carriers, they can still marry and have healthy children. There are tests during pregnancy that tell whether the baby is affected, so the couple can make an informed choice.

# Blood transfusion

For thalassemia major, regular blood transfusion is the main treatment. It does two jobs. It gives the body the hemoglobin it cannot make, so the child has energy and can grow. And it quietens down the bone marrow, which otherwise works so hard to make red cells that it widens the bones, especially of the face and skull.

Transfusion is usually started when tests confirm thalassemia major and the hemoglobin keeps falling, or the child is not growing well. After that, the child receives blood at regular intervals, in the most severe type about once a month. Before each transfusion the doctor checks the hemoglobin, and aims to keep it above a set target level all the time, not just to top it up when the child looks pale. Guidelines give exact targets, and the detailed transfusion chapter will list them.

Each transfusion takes a few hours, usually in a day care unit. The blood should be properly cross matched and screened for infections. Many centres use filtered blood to reduce reactions. Your team will keep a record of every unit given.

# Complications, and why too little blood is harmful

Many families, worried about cost or travel, stretch the gap between transfusions, or accept a low hemoglobin because the child "seems fine". This causes real harm over time.

When a child with thalassemia major gets too little blood:

- the bone marrow expands to make more red cells, which changes the shape of the face and skull and weakens the bones
- the spleen grows large, can cause a heavy, swollen tummy, and may start destroying blood, so even more transfusion is needed
- growth slows and puberty can be delayed
- the heart works harder all the time and can be strained
- the body absorbs more iron from food, adding to iron overload

Other complications can come from the disease itself or from the iron that builds up with treatment. These include heart problems, diabetes, thyroid and other hormone problems, delayed growth and puberty, weak bones (osteoporosis), gallstones, liver damage, blood clots, leg ulcers, and infections, especially after the spleen has been removed.

Most of these can be prevented or caught early with the right transfusion, chelation and regular check ups. A detailed chapter going through each complication is being added to this site.

# Chelation: removing the extra iron

Every bag of blood brings iron with it, and the body has no natural way to get rid of extra iron. Over months and years it piles up in the heart, the liver and the glands that make hormones. Iron overload is the main cause of serious complications in people who receive regular blood.

Chelation means taking a medicine that binds the extra iron so the body can pass it out in urine or stool. There are three chelation medicines: deferasirox and deferiprone, which are taken by mouth, and desferrioxamine, which is given as a slow injection under the skin. Some people need two of them together. Your hematologist chooses which one, the dose and the timing, based on the iron level and how the body responds.

Iron is checked with a blood test called serum ferritin and, where available, a special MRI scan called T2* that measures iron in the heart and liver.

Chelation only works if it is taken every day as advised. Missing it is one of the biggest risks for people with thalassemia. A detailed chapter on chelation, the tests and the side effects to report is being added to this site.

# Overall care

Thalassemia major needs lifelong care from a team, ideally at a thalassemia centre or with a hematologist who sees it often. Beyond transfusion and chelation, regular check ups usually include:

- iron levels, about every 3 months
- liver tests, about every 6 months
- tests for infections that can pass through blood, every year
- hormone tests, including growth, thyroid and blood sugar, every year
- heart checks
- growth charts in children

Vaccines matter, especially hepatitis B, pneumococcus, meningitis and the yearly flu vaccine, and even more so if the spleen has been removed. A balanced diet without extra iron supplements is usually advised. Children should go to school, play and live as normally as possible. Young adults can marry and many can have children, with careful planning.

For some children, a bone marrow (stem cell) transplant from a matched donor can cure thalassemia. It carries real risks, so it is weighed carefully for each child.

Living with thalassemia is hard on the whole family. Talking to other families, patient groups and counsellors helps. A detailed chapter on everyday care, covering follow up, vaccines, food, school, marriage, transplant and support, is being added to this site.

# Go to a hospital now if

- there is fever, especially if the spleen has been removed
- the person is breathless at rest, has chest pain, a racing or irregular heartbeat, or fainting
- there is fever, chills, back pain, rash, breathlessness or dark urine during or soon after a transfusion
- the eyes turn suddenly more yellow, or there is severe tummy pain
- there is sudden weakness of one side of the body, difficulty speaking, or a severe headache
- a leg becomes painful and swollen
- the person is very pale, drowsy or unusually weak before the next transfusion is due

# Myths and facts

## "Thalassemia spreads by touching or eating together"

No. It is inherited through genes from both parents. A child with thalassemia can sit, eat and play with anyone.

## "A carrier is ill and needs iron tablets"

A carrier is healthy. Their red cells are small, which can look like iron deficiency on a report, but iron does not help and can harm if not needed. A ferritin test can tell the difference.

## "If our first child has thalassemia, the next ones will be fine"

The 1 in 4 chance applies to every pregnancy, separately. Testing during each pregnancy is the only way to know.

## "A child with thalassemia cannot have a normal life"

With regular transfusion, chelation and check ups, children with thalassemia major go to school, work and marry. The care is demanding, but the outlook has improved greatly.

## "Stopping blood for a while will make the body make its own"

It will not. In thalassemia major the body cannot make enough working hemoglobin. Delaying transfusion only lets the marrow, spleen and bones suffer.

# Common questions

## What is thalassemia in simple words?

Thalassemia is an inherited blood condition in which the body makes too little hemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen. The result is anaemia. It ranges from a harmless carrier state to a serious condition needing regular blood.

## Can thalassemia be cured?

A bone marrow or stem cell transplant from a matched donor can cure it, but it carries risks and is not suitable for everyone. Most people live with thalassemia through regular transfusion, chelation and check ups.

## Can two thalassemia carriers marry?

Yes. They can marry and have healthy children. Each pregnancy has a 1 in 4 chance of thalassemia major, so they should see a doctor before pregnancy and have the baby tested during each pregnancy.

## How long do people with thalassemia major live?

With good modern treatment, many people with severe thalassemia now live into their 50s, 60s and beyond. In the past, it was often fatal by early adulthood. Regular transfusion and chelation make the biggest difference.

## Is thalassemia minor dangerous?

Thalassemia minor, or carrier state, is not dangerous and usually needs no treatment. The main reason to know is so you can check your partner and plan pregnancies.

## How often is blood needed in thalassemia major?

In the most severe type, about once a month, sometimes more often in older children and adults. The exact gap depends on keeping the hemoglobin above the target your doctor sets.

## Which test shows thalassemia?

A complete blood count gives the first clue, small red cells. HPLC or hemoglobin electrophoresis then confirms the type. Genetic tests can be done when needed, including during pregnancy.

# The main points

- Thalassemia is inherited, not infectious. A child gets thalassemia major only when both parents are carriers.
- Carriers are healthy, but should know their status before marriage or pregnancy.
- Thalassemia major needs regular blood transfusion, kept above a target hemoglobin, not just given when the child looks pale.
- Chelation every day removes the iron that transfusions bring. Missing it causes most serious complications.
- With regular care, people with thalassemia go to school, work, marry and live long lives.
