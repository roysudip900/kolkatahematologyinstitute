> A bone marrow or stem cell transplant replaces unhealthy blood-forming cells with healthy ones, from the person's own body or from a donor. It can cure or control some blood cancers, thalassemia, sickle cell disease and aplastic anaemia, but it is a long and demanding treatment with serious risks, so the decision is made with a hematologist and a transplant team, and recovery can take up to a year.

# What a transplant is

Stem cells are young cells in the bone marrow, inside the bones, that grow into every kind of blood cell. A transplant replaces stem cells that are not working, or are making unhealthy cells, with healthy ones.

- In an autologous transplant, the person's own stem cells are collected and given back later.
- In an allogeneic transplant, the stem cells come from a donor.

Before the new cells go in, chemotherapy or radiotherapy destroys the unhealthy blood cells and makes space. The new stem cells then travel to the bone marrow and begin making healthy blood cells.

# Who it is for

A transplant can be part of treatment for:

- some blood cancers, such as acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, acute myeloid leukaemia, Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and myeloma
- thalassemia and sickle cell disease
- aplastic anaemia

It aims to cure the condition or keep it under control. It is not the right choice for everyone. The team weighs the illness, the person's age and health, and whether there is a matching donor, against the risks.

# Finding a donor

Donated stem cells need to match the person's tissue type, so that the transplant has a better chance of working. A blood test checks the match, called HLA typing.

- The best match is usually a brother or sister, or sometimes another close family member.
- If no one in the family matches, a donor may be found through a registry of volunteers who are not related, or from stored umbilical cord blood.

For a donor, cells are usually collected from the blood. Injections for a few days increase the stem cells in the blood, then a machine collects them and returns the rest of the blood. Sometimes cells are taken from the hip bone with a needle, under general anaesthetic, and the hip may be sore afterwards.

# Before the transplant

- Tests check that the body is strong enough: heart tests such as an ECG and an echocardiogram, an X-ray or CT scan, blood tests for the liver and kidneys, and a bone marrow test in cancer.
- Staying as healthy as possible while waiting helps recovery. Some hospitals offer advice on food, exercise and mental health before a transplant.
- Chemotherapy and radiotherapy can affect fertility. Ask about storing eggs or sperm before treatment.

# The transplant, step by step

## Collecting the stem cells

For an autologous transplant, the person's own stem cells are collected in the same way as from a donor, usually from the blood with a machine.

## Conditioning

Chemotherapy or radiotherapy, called conditioning, destroys the unhealthy blood cells. It usually lasts one to ten days, sometimes longer. It also weakens the body's defences, so the person stays in a room of their own, with doors and windows closed, and everyone who comes in wears a mask, gloves and a gown.

## The transplant day

The stem cells are given through a thin tube placed in the chest or upper arm. They go into the blood, a bit like a blood transfusion. It is not an operation, it is not painful, and the person is awake.

## Waiting for the new cells to grow

The person stays in hospital for several weeks, in their own room, until the new cells start working and the team is sure it is safe to go home. The blood counts are checked often to see whether the new marrow is growing.

# The months after

Full recovery can take up to a year, and the team keeps watching for a year or more.

The care team will:

- give medicines to protect against infections
- give medicine to stop the immune system attacking the donor's cells, after a donor transplant
- arrange blood or platelet transfusions until the body makes enough of its own
- offer counselling and support from other patients

At home:

- Keep every check-up. They are usually weekly at first.
- Wash hands well and often. Wash fruit and vegetables well, and follow the team's advice about foods to avoid, such as undercooked eggs.
- Eat small meals regularly, rest when needed, and build up slowly with short walks.
- Protect the skin from the sun.
- Avoid a lot of alcohol, and travel only when the team says it is safe.
- Ask family and friends for help with everyday tasks.

A transplant asks a lot of a family, through the weeks in hospital and the months of recovery at home. Ask the team early what help and support the patient will need, and who in the family can give it.

# The risks

The chemotherapy or radiotherapy and the transplant itself can cause:

- hair loss for a while, a sore mouth and throat, nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea
- tiredness, anaemia, and bleeding or bruising easily
- infections, which can be serious
- blood clots
- anxiety or low mood
- problems having children later

After a donor transplant, the donor's cells can attack the body. This is called graft-versus-host disease (GvHD). It can start soon after the transplant or much later, and can affect the skin, gut, liver, mouth, eyes, lungs and joints. Its signs include a red itchy rash, diarrhoea and tummy cramps, mouth ulcers, yellow eyes or skin, and dry, sore eyes. Sometimes the body rejects the donor cells, which is very serious.

Some of these complications can be very serious, and some people die from them. The team explains the risks for each person before the transplant, and treats side effects as they come.

# Go to a hospital now if

Go at once, or call the transplant team straight away, and do not drive yourself, if someone who has had a transplant has:

- a fever, or feels hot, cold, sweaty or shivery
- other signs of infection, such as a sore throat, cough, diarrhoea, or pain when passing urine
- pain, swelling and warmth in a leg or arm
- sudden breathlessness, sharp chest pain, or coughing up blood
- difficulty breathing or wheezing
- bleeding that will not stop, or many new bruises
- yellow eyes or skin, a spreading rash, or severe diarrhoea

# Take these questions to your doctor

- Is a transplant the right treatment for this illness, and what are the other choices?
- Will it use my own cells or a donor's, and who should be tested as a donor?
- How long will we be in hospital, and how long near the transplant centre afterwards?
- What are the main risks for this person?
- How can we store eggs or sperm before treatment?
- What will we need at home, and who should stay with the patient?

# Common questions

## Is a bone marrow transplant an operation?

No. The new stem cells are given through a thin tube into the blood, like a transfusion. It is not painful and the person is awake.

## Is donating stem cells harmful for a brother or sister?

Cells are usually collected from the blood with a machine after a few days of injections. If they are taken from the hip bone, it is done under general anaesthetic and the hip may be sore afterwards. The team explains the donor's care too.

## Does a transplant always cure?

No. It aims to cure or control the illness, and it works for many people, but not for everyone. The team explains the chances for each person.
